About This Skipper

Half Butterfly, Half Bullet

Skippers are the stocky, fast, moth-like cousins of the butterflies — big-eyed, thick-bodied, with hooked antennae and a darting flight that gave the family its name. The Silver-spotted Skipper is the largest and most conspicuous of the forty-odd species in Ohio, and the one most people notice: a chocolate-brown butterfly with a translucent gold band across each forewing and, on the underside of the hindwing, a big, ragged patch of silver-white that flashes as it perches.

It almost never rests with its wings open. Instead it sits with them closed over its back, or in the half-open "jet plane" pose of the spread-wing skippers, and launches off in a blur to chase another skipper, a bee, or anything else that passes. It is a creature of sunny edges — meadow margins, garden borders, wood openings — wherever its legume host plants and its favourite flowers meet.

The caterpillar lives on legumes — honey locust and black locust above all, with false indigo, tick-trefoils, and hog peanut — and builds itself a folded leaf shelter to hide in by day. The adult has a strong preference for blue, purple, pink, and red flowers, and will pass over most yellow ones.

What Brings Them In

Legumes to Raise Them, Purple Flowers to Feed Them

The caterpillars need legumes: honey locust is the host tree on this site, and black locust, false indigo, and wild tick-trefoils do the same job elsewhere. The adults are choosy nectar feeders with a documented bias toward blue, purple, pink, and red flowers — the blazing stars, bergamots, and ironweeds of the summer border — and are rarely seen on yellow ones. They share these blooms with the Monarch and the bumble bee. Tap any plant to read its profile.

Life Cycle

From Egg to Wing

Late spring
Adults emerge in May from pupae that overwintered in the leaf litter. Females lay single green eggs on or near legume leaves — sometimes on a neighbouring plant, leaving the hatchling to walk to its host.
Early summer
Each caterpillar cuts and folds a flap of leaf, silks it into a shelter, and hides there by day, coming out to feed at night. As it grows it builds bigger shelters, eventually tying several leaflets together.
Midsummer
The second, larger brood flies from July into September — the peak of skipper activity, with males perched on prominent leaves and darting at anything that passes their patch of sun.
Late summer
Late caterpillars finish feeding and pupate inside a final leaf shelter, which falls with the leaves or is spun among debris on the ground.
Fall–winter
The pupa waits out the winter in leaf litter beneath its host plant, wrapped in silk and dead leaves — which is why an un-raked edge under a honey locust matters.

OverwinteringLeaf litter and debris under and near the host plants, where the pupa passes the winter
What harmsInsecticides, tidy fall clean-up under host trees, and gardens with no purple or pink bloom in midsummer

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

The Silver-spotted Skipper is easy to please, and much of what it needs is already on this site.

Plant for purple and pink. Blazing star, wild bergamot, ironweed, Joe-Pye weed, and blue vervain are exactly its colours. A border built for skippers looks like a midsummer prairie in bloom.

Value the legumes. A honey locust or black locust nearby is the nursery. Where there is no room for a tree, false indigo and native tick-trefoils in a sunny bed raise caterpillars too.

Read the folded leaves. A leaflet folded over and stitched shut is a caterpillar's home, not disease. Leave it, and look in from the side for the big red-brown head.

Leave the litter under host trees. Pupae overwinter in fallen leaves. Raking clean under a honey locust in October removes next May's first brood.

Give them a perch. A tall flower head or a shrub tip in the sun is where a male sets up shop. It is also the easiest place to watch one.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Skipper

01

The Caterpillar That Throws Its Droppings

A caterpillar hiding in a folded leaf faces a problem: its droppings pile up in the shelter and their smell can draw parasitic wasps straight to it. The Silver-spotted Skipper solves this by launching them. A blood-pressure catch in the hind end flicks each pellet away at high speed — up to about five feet, or nearly forty body-lengths — in a behaviour biologists have measured with high-speed cameras. It is the insect equivalent of taking the garbage a very long way from the house.

02

Not Quite a Butterfly

Skippers sit in their own family, the Hesperiidae, between butterflies and moths. They have the butterfly's day-flying habit and clubbed antennae, but the club is hooked back at the tip, the body is thick and furry, and the flight is a fast, low, skipping dash rather than a flutter. Their caterpillars build shelters like moth larvae do. There are more than forty species in Ohio, most of them small and orange and very hard to tell apart; this one is the exception.

03

A Taste for Purple

Most butterflies visit whatever is in bloom. The Silver-spotted Skipper is unusual in having a measurable colour preference: field studies find it on blue, purple, pink, and red flowers far more often than on yellow or orange ones, which it will fly past even when they are abundant. Nobody is quite sure why. The practical result is that a garden of blazing star and bergamot will have skippers, and a garden of black-eyed Susans and coreopsis mostly will not.

04

A Face to Scare Birds

The caterpillar's head is enormous, dark red-brown, and marked with two large orange eyespots, so that from the shelter's opening it looks like something much larger staring out. Disturbed, it will rear back and regurgitate a bitter green fluid. Between the hidden shelter, the flung droppings, and the false face, a soft, slow, leaf-eating larva manages to reach adulthood in a world full of birds and wasps.

Skippers & Their Doubles

The silver patch settles most sightings, but Ohio has a few other large brown skippers, and a swarm of small orange ones that share the family's shape and darting flight.

Grass Skippers

Peck's, Zabulon, Least & others

The dozens of small orange-and-brown skippers of meadows and lawns, whose caterpillars eat grasses. Half the size, and they perch in the "jet plane" pose with hindwings flat and forewings raised. The "Skippers" listed across the plant pages are mostly these.
