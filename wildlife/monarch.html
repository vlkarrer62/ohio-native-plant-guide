About This Butterfly

The Milkweed Butterfly

The Monarch is the best-known butterfly in North America and, for most Ohioans, the one they learned first: broad orange wings ruled with black veins, a wide black border dotted with two rows of white spots, and a black body flecked with white. It is a large, unhurried flier, gliding on flat wings between flowers with a few slow beats, and it is impossible to mistake for anything but its mimic, the smaller viceroy.

What sets the Monarch apart is not its looks but its life. Its caterpillars eat milkweed and nothing else, storing the plant's bitter toxins so that both caterpillar and butterfly are distasteful to birds. And no Monarch that emerges in Ohio in late summer will die here: the year's final generation flies some two thousand miles to a handful of mountain forests in central Mexico, winters there in the millions, and starts the return in spring. Their grandchildren and great-grandchildren are the Monarchs you see next summer.

Everything comes back to milkweed. Without it there are no eggs, no caterpillars, and no next generation — and without late-blooming nectar in September, the migrants cannot fuel the flight south. An Ohio garden can supply both.

What Brings Them In

Milkweed to Raise Them, Nectar to Fuel Them

A Monarch garden has two jobs. The first is milkweed — the only plants the caterpillars can eat, and the plants females search for when they arrive in late spring. The second is a long run of nectar, with special weight on September bloom: the migrating generation must build fat reserves before the flight south, and goldenrods, asters, and ironweed are what they find in Ohio when they need it. They share many of these blooms with the tiger swallowtail and the silver-spotted skipper. Tap any plant to read its profile.

Life Cycle

From Egg to Mexico

Late spring
The first Monarchs reach Ohio in late May and June — not the butterflies that left Mexico, but their children, born on milkweed in Texas and the South. Females lay single pale eggs on the undersides of milkweed leaves.
Summer
Eggs hatch in about four days. The banded caterpillar eats milkweed for two weeks through five moults, then hangs from a silk pad and forms the jade-green chrysalis. Ten to fourteen days later a new adult emerges. Two or three generations follow, each living only a few weeks.
Late summer
The last generation emerges in August and early September physically different: it does not breed, lives eight months instead of a few weeks, and turns south. Numbers peak in Ohio gardens and along the Lake Erie shore in mid-September.
Fall
Migrants ride cold fronts south at up to fifty miles a day, nectaring on goldenrod and aster as they go. By November they have gathered in a few high oyamel fir forests in the mountains of Michoacán.
Winter–spring
Clustered by the millions on the fir boughs, they wait out the winter in near-dormancy. In March they mate, fly north into Texas, lay eggs on the first milkweed, and die. The cycle back to Ohio takes their descendants.

Habitat & Needs

What It Needs

Adult foodNectar from open, sunny flowers — milkweeds, blazing star, bergamot, coneflower, and above all the goldenrods, asters, and ironweed of late summer
Caterpillar foodMilkweed leaves only. In Ohio chiefly common, swamp, and butterfly milkweed; native species are far better than tropical milkweed, which can disrupt migration
SeasonMilkweed in leaf from late May through September; nectar unbroken from June to the first frost
SpaceSunny, open ground — meadows, roadsides, and gardens; females find milkweed more easily in patches than as single plants
MigrationRoost trees and fall nectar along the route; Lake Erie's shoreline funnels migrants and concentrates them in September
WinterIntact oyamel fir forest in Mexico — the one need no Ohio garden can meet, and the one that decides the species' future
What harmsInsecticides, especially systemics in nursery plants; loss of milkweed to mowing and herbicide; a September with nothing in bloom

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

Few garden visitors give such a clear return on so simple a set of choices. Every one of these is within reach of a single yard.

Plant native milkweed, and more than one. Swamp milkweed for moist ground and butterfly weed for dry are both on this site. A cluster of several plants is found more readily than one, and survives caterpillars better.

Keep September in bloom. Goldenrod, New England aster, ironweed, and boneset are the migration's filling stations. A garden that stops flowering in August has fed the summer generations and abandoned the one that matters most.

Never spray milkweed. Aphids on milkweed are normal and harmless; anything that kills them kills Monarch caterpillars too. Ask nurseries whether plants were treated with systemic insecticides before buying.

Leave the leaves and stems. Chewed, ragged milkweed is a working plant. Cut it back only in late winter, and skip tropical milkweed, which stays green into fall and can hold butterflies back from migrating.

Count what you see. Monarch monitoring programs rely on gardeners' records of eggs, caterpillars, and September flights; the data shapes how the migration is protected.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Butterfly

01

A Migration No Individual Completes

The Monarch is the only butterfly known to make a regular two-way migration like a bird, but no single Monarch makes the round trip. The butterfly that leaves Ohio in September flies to Mexico, winters, and gets as far back as Texas in spring before dying. Its children move north, and its grandchildren or great-grandchildren arrive in Ohio in June — navigating to a forest none of them has ever seen, using the sun and an internal magnetic compass.

02

Armed by Milkweed

Milkweed sap is loaded with cardenolides, heart-poisons that make most animals sick. Monarch caterpillars not only tolerate them but store them, and carry them through the chrysalis into the adult. A bird that eats a Monarch usually vomits and learns to avoid orange-and-black butterflies for life. The viceroy, which looks almost identical, was long thought to be a harmless copy; it turns out to be unpalatable in its own right, so the two species share the cost of teaching birds.

03

The Lake Erie Funnel

Migrating Monarchs will not cross open water if they can help it. Those moving south through Ontario and Michigan pile up along the north shore of Lake Erie and cross at its narrowest points, then follow the Ohio shoreline. On the right September day, after a cold front, thousands can be seen streaming past headlands and roosting in lakeside trees — one of the great wildlife spectacles Ohio has to offer.

04

A Butterfly Under Watch

The eastern Monarch population is measured each winter by the area of Mexican forest it covers, and that figure has fallen by well over half since the 1990s, driven by milkweed loss across the farm belt, logging at the wintering sites, and extreme weather. In December 2024 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing the Monarch as a threatened species. Milkweed in gardens will not solve that alone, but it is one of the few conservation actions that anyone with a sunny patch of ground can take.

Monarchs & Their Doubles

Orange-and-black is a warning colour worn by several Ohio butterflies. The Monarch's size, slow glide, and plain black veins usually settle it, but one mimic is close enough to fool almost everyone.
