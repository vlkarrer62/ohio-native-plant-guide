About This Cicada

The Sound of Summer Heat

The dog days of summer — the sweltering weeks of late July and August — are named for the Dog Star, Sirius, but in Ohio they sound like cicadas. The long, rising, sawing drone from the treetops on a hot afternoon is the song of the annual cicadas, a handful of species in the genus Neotibicen that appear in Ohio every summer: the swamp cicada, Linne's cicada, the scissor-grinder, the lyric cicada, and the one usually called the dog-day cicada. They differ in song and detail, and to almost everyone they are simply "the cicadas."

They are big, blunt, heavy-bodied insects, patterned in green, black, and brown to vanish against bark, with wide-set eyes and two pairs of long, glassy wings held roof-like over the body. They are not locusts — locusts are grasshoppers — and they do not bite, sting, or swarm. What they do is sing, and they do it louder than almost any other insect on earth.

Every cicada you hear spent years underground, feeding on the roots of the trees above it, before climbing out for a few weeks of sound and sun. A yard with a summer chorus is a yard whose trees and soil have been left alone long enough for the nymphs to grow up.

Habitat Associations

Trees Above, Roots Below

A cicada's life is bound to trees at both ends: the nymph drinks from the roots for years, and the adult climbs the trunk to moult, sings from the branches, and lays its eggs in the twigs. Oaks, maples, hickories, honey locust, and most other natives all serve. It shares the treetops with the katydid, which takes over the chorus when the cicadas fall silent at dusk. Life Cycle

Years Below, Weeks Above

Late summer
A mated female cuts a row of slits in a pencil-thick twig with her blade-like ovipositor and lays eggs in each. The twig tip may wilt and brown — the harmless "flagging" seen on trees in August.
Early fall
Eggs hatch after six to ten weeks. The ant-sized nymphs drop to the ground and dig in, finding a rootlet to feed on.
2–5 years
Underground the nymph drinks dilute xylem sap from tree roots, growing through five stages and moving to bigger roots as it grows. Because the species' generations overlap, some emerge every year.
July–August
On a warm night the full-grown nymph tunnels up, climbs the nearest trunk, splits its skin down the back, and pulls free — soft, pale, and green — leaving the brown husk on the bark. By morning the wings have hardened.
August–Sept.
Adults live two to six weeks. Males sing through the hottest hours, females fly to them, and the cycle turns over. The chorus thins in September and the last stragglers are gone by early October.

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

Cicadas ask for patience more than planting. The nymph that emerges this August hatched from an egg laid several summers ago, so a yard's cicada chorus is a record of how it has been treated for years.

Keep the big trees. Every mature oak, maple, or locust in a neighbourhood is a cicada nursery. Removing one removes the nymphs on its roots along with it.

Leave the ground under trees alone. Nymphs cannot dig through pavement or survive repeated tilling. Mulched beds, leaf litter, and lawn all work; patios and compacted parking do not.

Skip the grub treatments. Lawn insecticides applied for grubs soak into the same soil the nymphs live in. A yard treated every year has fewer cicadas every year.

Ignore the flagging. Brown twig tips in late summer are egg-laying scars and do no lasting harm to established trees. Only newly planted saplings need protecting, and a mesh cover does it.

Leave the shells. The brown husks on trunks and stems are the best evidence of a healthy population, and children have been collecting them for generations. Nothing needs to be cleaned up.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Cicada

01

Drums, Not Wings

Unlike katydids and crickets, cicadas do not rub anything together. A male has a pair of ribbed membranes called tymbals on the sides of his abdomen; muscles buckle each rib in and out hundreds of times a second, and a hollow abdomen amplifies the clicks into a continuous buzz. The loudest species reach over a hundred decibels at close range — as loud as a chainsaw, and among the loudest sounds made by any insect. The male folds down his own eardrums while he sings so he does not deafen himself.

02

Annual, But Not One-Year

The "annual" cicadas are called that because some appear every year, not because they live only a year. Each nymph spends two to five years underground; the population is simply made of overlapping generations, so a few of each cohort emerge every summer. Ohio's periodical cicadas are the exception — every nymph in a brood emerges together after exactly seventeen years, in numbers that can reach a million an acre. Ohio hosts several broods on staggered schedules, so a periodical emergence reaches some part of the state every few years.

03

The Wasp That Hunts Them

In late July, big black-and-yellow wasps nearly two inches long begin patrolling the trees. The cicada killer is a solitary wasp that paralyses a singing cicada with its sting, hauls the heavier insect back to a burrow in bare, sandy ground, and lays an egg on it as food for a single grub. Despite their size the wasps are placid and almost never sting people; a cicada killer digging in the garden is a sign that the cicada population is healthy enough to hunt.

04

The Dog Days

The ancient Greeks and Romans blamed the hottest stretch of summer on Sirius, the Dog Star, which rises with the sun in late July and was thought to add its heat to the day's. The "dog days" have kept the name for two thousand years, and in North America the cicadas that emerge in that window inherited it. Their song is so tied to heat that it starts and stops with the temperature: a cloud crossing the sun can silence a whole tree, and the first hot morning brings it back.

Dog-day Cicadas

Neotibicen spp.

The green, black, and brown cicadas of July to September, with dark eyes and clear, green-veined wings. Several species share Ohio: the swamp cicada sings in the morning, Linne's and the scissor-grinder in the afternoon and evening. Present every year.

Periodical Cicada

Magicicada spp.

Smaller, jet black with bright red eyes and orange wing veins, emerging in vast synchronised broods in May and June — a month or more before the dog-day cicadas start. Ohio has several broods on separate schedules.
