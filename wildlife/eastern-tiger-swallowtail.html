About This Butterfly

Ohio's Great Yellow Butterfly

The Eastern Tiger Swallowtail is among the largest and most familiar butterflies of the eastern United States, and one of the easiest to know on sight: broad yellow wings crossed by four black "tiger stripes" on each forewing, a black border set with a row of small yellow spots, and long tails trailing from the hindwings. Near each tail sits a blush of orange and a line of blue. It ranges across the east and north into southern Ontario, and in a garden it tends to sail high, then drop to feed.

Males are always yellow. Females come in two forms — a yellow one much like the male but with more blue along the hindwing, and a dark form that is nearly all black. The butterfly you're most likely to see drifting over an Ohio garden in summer is this one, whether visiting flowers for nectar or gliding along the sunny edge of the trees where its caterpillars feed.

It lives in two worlds at once: as an adult it drinks nectar from open garden flowers, but as a caterpillar it eats tree leaves — chiefly wild cherry and tuliptree. A garden that helps it needs both: flowers to feed the butterfly, and the right trees nearby to raise the next generation.

What Brings Them In

Trees to Raise Them, Flowers to Feed Them

Supporting this butterfly means thinking about both halves of its life. The caterpillars are leaf-eaters of a few native trees — most reliably wild black cherry (Prunus serotina) and tuliptree (Liriodendron tulipifera), sometimes ash or aspen. These often aren't garden plantings, but a neighbourhood with cherries and tuliptrees quietly raises far more swallowtails than any one flower bed. The adults, by contrast, nectar widely, and are easy to feed. They share many of these blooms with the carpenter bee and bumble bee. Tap any plant to read its profile.

Life Cycle

From Egg to Wing

Spring
Adults emerge from chrysalises that overwintered, and the first flight begins. Females lay pale green eggs singly on the leaves of host trees.
Early summer
Caterpillars hatch and feed on tree leaves, growing through several stages. Young ones resemble bird droppings; older ones turn green with large false eyespots.
Midsummer
A second brood of adults is on the wing across Ohio — the busiest stretch, with butterflies nectaring widely in gardens and along sunny edges.
Late summer
The season's last caterpillars finish feeding. Late-brood pupae enter diapause — a paused, dormant chrysalis that will wait out the winter.
Fall–winter
The butterfly passes the cold as a chrysalis, often attached to a twig or tucked in leaf litter and debris, holding until spring warmth releases the next generation.

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

An Eastern Tiger Swallowtail garden feeds the adult and makes room for the whole life cycle — and much of it is about doing a little less.

Bloom across the season. Two broods means two windows to feed: spring flowers for the first, and a strong run of summer-into-fall nectar for the second. Aim for something open at every stage.

Value the host trees. Wild cherry and tuliptree are what the caterpillars actually eat. Where there's room, they're worth keeping or planting; where there isn't, a neighbourhood that holds onto them does the work.

Leave a damp, sunny spot. A shallow saucer of wet sand or a patch of mud in the sun gives puddling males a place to gather minerals.

Go easy on fall clean-up. Chrysalises overwinter on twigs and in leaf litter. Raking and clearing every corner can remove next year's butterflies along with the leaves.

Skip the insecticides. Sprays kill caterpillars and adults alike, and a butterfly garden and a sprayed garden rarely coexist.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Butterfly

01

A Female in Disguise

Male tiger swallowtails are always yellow. Females come in two forms — one yellow like the male, and one almost entirely black. That dark form is thought to be a case of Batesian mimicry: it resembles the pipevine swallowtail, a butterfly that is distasteful to birds, and so may borrow some of that protection. The dark form is strictly female, carried on a gene that males don't inherit — so every dark tiger swallowtail you see is a she.

02

A Caterpillar That Plays Snake

The caterpillar defends itself by pretending to be something it isn't. When small, it's mottled brown and white and looks convincingly like a bird dropping — not worth a second glance. As it grows it turns green and develops two large false eyespots near the head, giving it a startling, snake-like face. Disturbed, it can also evert a forked orange gland, the osmeterium, that gives off a strong smell to push predators away.

03

Two Lives in Two Places

Few garden visitors live so plainly in two worlds. As a caterpillar, this butterfly is bound to a handful of trees, eating their leaves high in the canopy. As an adult, it's a creature of flowers, drifting between blooms for nectar. The trees and the flowers are usually different plants in different places — which is why a butterfly-friendly landscape is really a partnership between the garden and the trees around it.

04

The Boys at the Puddle

On a warm day you may find tiger swallowtails clustered at the damp edge of a puddle, a driveway seep, or wet sand. This behaviour, called puddling, is mostly the males: they draw up dissolved salts and minerals from the wet ground, nutrients they pass to females and that support the next generation. A reliably damp, sunny spot can turn a garden into a regular stop.

Dark-Morph Female

Papilio glaucus (f. nigra)

Not a different butterfly at all — a female Eastern Tiger Swallowtail in her black form, with the tiger stripes showing only as faint shadows and a strong blue wash on the hindwing. Thought to mimic the toxic pipevine swallowtail.

Northern Look-Alike

Canadian Tiger Swallowtail

Papilio canadensis

A very similar but more northern, boreal species that reaches only the edge of the region — smaller, with broader black stripes and a continuous yellow band on the underside of the forewing, and typically a single brood. Uncommon this far south.

The Toxic Model

Pipevine Swallowtail

Battus philenor

A dark swallowtail with an iridescent blue-green sheen on the hindwing, distasteful to birds because its caterpillars feed on pipevines. It's the butterfly the dark-morph female tiger swallowtail is thought to imitate for protection.
