About This Grasshopper

The Meadow's Grazer

Walk through any Ohio meadow, roadside, or unmown field in August and the ground ahead of you erupts with grasshoppers — and most of them are this one. The Red-legged Grasshopper is the commonest of the state's several dozen species: a neat, medium-sized insect in olive-brown and yellow, with a saddle-shaped plate behind the head, narrow wings folded along the back, and the long hind legs that give the group its name. Look closely at those legs and you will see a black herringbone on the thigh and a bright red shin below it.

Grasshoppers are the daytime, ground-level members of the singing insects. Their antennae are short — a third the length of a katydid's — their ears sit on the sides of the abdomen, and most of them, this species included, are nearly silent, making only a faint crackle in flight. What they do instead of sing is eat, and be eaten: they are the primary converters of meadow grass into the protein that grassland birds raise their young on.

A meadow's grasshoppers are its food supply. Kestrels, meadowlarks, bluebirds, wild turkeys, and nearly every songbird that nests in open country feed grasshoppers to their chicks, and the decline of grassland birds tracks the loss of the unmown, unsprayed grass that grasshoppers need.

Habitat Associations

Grass to Eat, Grass to Hide In

Grasshoppers are meadow animals through and through. They eat grasses first and broadleaf plants second, sun themselves on bare soil, lay their eggs in it, and hide from birds in standing stems. The warm-season grasses on this site are their habitat in every sense. They share the meadow with the meadow katydids and are hunted there by the lady beetle's larger neighbours — mantids, robber flies, and spiders. Tap any plant to read its profile.

Life Cycle

From Egg Pod to Meadow

Winter
The species overwinters as eggs. Each female pushed her abdomen an inch into bare soil in fall and laid twenty to thirty eggs in a frothy case that hardens into a pod.
Late spring
Eggs hatch in late May and June as the soil warms. The nymphs are tiny copies of the adults without wings, and begin feeding on tender grass at once.
Early summer
Five or six moults over six to eight weeks. Wing pads appear and lengthen with each stage; the nymphs sun on bare soil to speed their growth.
Late summer
Adults from July on, with numbers peaking in August and September — the height of the meadow's grasshopper crop, and the season when grassland birds fatten their broods on them.
Fall
Mating and egg-laying through September and October, on warm afternoons on bare ground. The adults keep feeding until a hard frost kills them; the eggs carry on.

PredatorsKestrels, meadowlarks, bluebirds, turkeys, and most open-country songbirds; also mantids, robber flies, spiders, and parasitic flies
What harmsFrequent mowing, insecticides, and the conversion of meadow to close-cut lawn or pavement; tilling destroys egg pods

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

Grasshoppers are the one insect on this site that nobody sets out to attract, and the one that grassland birds cannot do without. Supporting them means letting a piece of ground be a meadow.

Leave grass standing. A patch of native grass left unmown from spring to the following spring is a complete grasshopper habitat: food, cover, basking stems, and egg sites in the soil beneath.

Mow in rotation, not all at once. If a meadow must be cut, cut a third of it each year in late winter. A single late-summer mowing destroys a season's grasshoppers and the birds' food with them.

Keep some bare soil. Egg pods go into open ground in the sun. A meadow with no gaps in the thatch has nowhere to lay; a few patches of bare earth or a gravel edge fix that.

Skip the sprays. A native meadow in Ohio does not produce damaging grasshopper numbers; the outbreaks of the western plains do not happen here. Insecticide kills the grasshoppers and the birds' meal in one pass.

Watch the birds. A kestrel hovering over a meadow, a bluebird dropping from a fence post, a turkey hen leading poults through tall grass — each is hunting grasshoppers. They are the reason the meadow matters.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Grasshopper

01

The Meadow's Protein

By weight, grasshoppers are among the most abundant animals in a summer meadow, and they are the single most important food for many grassland birds. An American kestrel may take dozens a day; eastern bluebirds, meadowlarks, and wild turkey poults are raised almost entirely on them. Studies of declining grassland birds keep returning to the same finding: fewer insects in the fields, and grasshoppers first among them. A yard that holds a grasshopper meadow is holding a food supply.

02

Ears on the Belly, Spit in the Mouth

Grasshoppers hear through a pair of oval eardrums on the sides of the first abdominal segment, tucked under the wing bases — not on the legs like a katydid. Picked up, most will produce a drop of brown fluid from the mouth, the "tobacco juice" every child who has caught one knows. It is regurgitated gut contents, bitter and harmless, and it makes birds drop them.

03

Built to Leap

The hind legs that mark the group are spring-loaded catapults. A grasshopper crouches, locks the leg joint, builds tension in the femur muscles, and releases — launching itself twenty times its body length in a fraction of a second, faster than muscle alone could manage. Once airborne the wings open, and a startled red-legged grasshopper can fly thirty or forty feet before dropping back into the grass and vanishing.

04

Not a Locust

Cicadas are often miscalled locusts in Ohio, but the real locusts are grasshoppers — species that, under crowding, switch into a swarming form and migrate in the millions. North America's own, the Rocky Mountain locust, darkened the skies of the Great Plains in the 1870s and then went extinct within thirty years, probably when farming destroyed the river-valley soils where it laid its eggs. Ohio's grasshoppers, red-legged included, never swarm; they stay in their meadows and feed the birds.

Grasshoppers & Their Relatives
