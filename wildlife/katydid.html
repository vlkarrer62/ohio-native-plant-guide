About This Katydid

The Voice in the Oaks

Almost everyone in Ohio has heard a Common True Katydid; almost no one has seen one. From late July until frost, the loud, harsh, rhythmic "ka-ty-did, ka-ty-did-n't" that fills the trees after dark is this insect, and the reason it stays hidden is that it lives its whole adult life in the canopy — the tops of oaks, hickories, and other broadleaf trees — and comes down only by accident.

It is a plump, bright green insect nearly two inches long, with forewings that are shaped, veined, and even slightly cupped exactly like a pair of oak leaves held tented over its back. The hind legs are long and built for jumping; the antennae are thread-thin and longer than the body, which is what separates katydids from grasshoppers. Despite the wings, it is a poor flier and mostly walks, climbs, and hops. The wings are for singing.

Katydids turn oak leaves into protein. A summer's worth of them in a big oak feeds screech owls, bats, cuckoos, and nesting songbirds, and their eggs carry the population through winter. The chorus you hear is a census of the trees in your neighbourhood.

Habitat Associations

Trees to Live In, Meadows for the Cousins

The Common True Katydid is a creature of trees — oaks first, then hickories, maples, and most other broadleaf natives — and needs nothing from a flower bed. Its many smaller relatives are less particular: bush katydids sit in shrubs and tall perennials, and meadow katydids sing from grasses and sedges by day. Between them, the group is woven through every layer of this site. It shares the night with the firefly and the treetops with the cicada. Tap any plant to read its profile.

Life Cycle

From Egg to Chorus

Winter
The species passes the cold as flat, oval eggs, pressed by the female's blade-like ovipositor into crevices of bark and the soft tissue of twigs high in the trees.
Spring
Eggs hatch in May. The nymphs look like tiny wingless adults and begin feeding at once on the new leaves, climbing toward the canopy.
Early summer
Five or six moults over about two months. Wing pads appear and grow with each stage; the insects stay silent and nearly invisible among the leaves.
Late summer
Adults by late July. Males begin calling at dusk and continue all night; females answer with faint ticks and climb toward the loudest singers. Peak chorus in August and early September.
Fall
Mated females lay eggs through September and October. The chorus slows with the cooling nights, drops to a single lazy pulse, and stops with the first hard frost, which kills the adults.

Habitat & Needs

What It Needs

FoodLeaves of broadleaf trees, especially oaks; also hickory, maple, and others. A few katydids in a mature tree do no measurable harm
HabitatThe upper canopy of deciduous woods, woodlots, parks, and tree-lined streets; anywhere with big trees, including cities
Egg sitesRough bark and living twigs of the host trees, where the female inserts eggs with her ovipositor
SoundWarm, humid, dark nights; the call rate is set by temperature and males synchronise with their neighbours
MovementPoor fliers, so a katydid population depends on connected trees; isolated street trees are colonised slowly
PredatorsScreech owls, bats, cuckoos, and other birds; katydids are a major summer food for nestlings and night hunters
What harmsCanopy loss, insecticide fogging for mosquitoes, and the removal of the rough-barked old trees that hold eggs

How to Support It

Garden Actions That Help

You cannot plant a katydid garden the way you plant a butterfly garden. What you can do is keep the trees, keep them unsprayed, and keep the night dark and quiet enough to hear them.

Plant and keep oaks. A mature oak is the single best thing for katydids, and for almost everything else on this site. Young oaks planted now are the chorus of thirty years from now.

Say no to mosquito fogging. Neighbourhood spraying for mosquitoes kills katydids, fireflies, and every other night insect in the trees. The chorus goes silent in a sprayed yard.

Leave twigs and rough bark. Eggs overwinter in living twigs and bark crevices. Heavy pruning in fall and winter removes them; prune lightly and leave the old, rough-barked trees standing.

Plant the understory too. Bush katydids use shrubs and tall perennials; meadow katydids need standing grass. A yard with all three layers has all three kinds of singer.

Listen. The Common True Katydid's call is so loud and regular that you can count the beat and read the temperature from it. On a warm August night, sit out with the lights off and listen for the synchronised choruses in neighbouring trees answering each other.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Katydid

01

A Song Played on Its Own Wings

A katydid's call is made by stridulation: a toothed file on the underside of one forewing is scraped across a hardened ridge on the other, and a taut, drum-like patch of wing membrane amplifies the result. The insect hears with a pair of slit-like ears on its front legs, just below the knees. Males in one tree fall into step with each other, and choruses in neighbouring trees alternate — the "did" and "didn't" of the folk rhyme are two groups answering.

02

The Thermometer in the Trees

Katydids and crickets are cold-blooded, so the speed of their song tracks the temperature. On a hot August night the Common True Katydid rasps its three pulses well over once a second; as the night cools the beat slows, and by the chilly evenings of October it may manage only a single drawn-out "kaaa-tyyy" every few seconds. The same physiology underlies the well-known cricket thermometer, and the katydid's slowing rhythm is the sound of the season winding down.

03

Wings for Singing, Not Flying

The Common True Katydid has full-sized wings and almost never uses them to fly. It is heavy, the wings are cupped and stiff, and a katydid that drops out of a tree flutters down like a leaf and then walks back up the trunk. The wings have been reshaped for two other jobs entirely: producing sound, and looking so exactly like oak leaves — veins, midrib, and all — that a bird can search a branch and never find it.

04

Katydid, Katy Didn't

The name comes from the call, and the call came with a story: a New England folktale about a girl named Katy, a jilted lover, and a chorus of insects that took sides, endlessly accusing and defending her — Katy did! Katy didn't! Oliver Wendell Holmes turned it into a poem in 1831, and the name stuck to the whole family, so that even the silent bush katydids and the day-singing meadow katydids carry a name earned by this one loud species.

Look-Alikes

Katydids & Their Relatives

Ohio has some two dozen katydids, most of them slimmer, quieter, and lower down than the Common True Katydid. The long antennae separate all of them from grasshoppers; wing shape and song sort the rest.
