About This Plant

The Tropical Tree of the Bottomlands

Pawpaw looks as if it wandered north from somewhere much warmer, and in a sense it did: it is the only temperate member of the custard-apple family, a tropical clan of soursops, cherimoyas, and ylang-ylang. In Ohio it grows in the rich, moist soil of river bottoms and ravine slopes, beneath sycamores and tuliptrees, usually not as a single tree but as a clonal thicket — a grove of slim grey trunks sent up from spreading root suckers, all one plant.

The leaves are the giveaway: up to a foot long, obovate (widest toward the tip), smooth-edged, and hanging from the twigs in a way that gives a pawpaw grove a drooping, layered, jungle-like look. In April, before or just as those leaves open, the bare twigs carry nodding maroon flowers with three outer and three inner petals, faintly scented of fermenting fruit. By September the few that were pollinated have become clusters of green-skinned fruit, the largest edible fruit native to North America, with soft yellow custard flesh around two rows of large brown seeds.

Pawpaw is the only larval host of the zebra swallowtail in Ohio — the long-tailed black-and-white butterfly of river valleys cannot exist without it. The same acetogenin compounds that keep deer and most insects off the leaves are carried into the caterpillars and make the butterflies distasteful to birds.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Mid-spring
Peak bloom in April and early May — velvety brown buds open into nodding maroon flowers along the bare twigs, visited by carrion flies and small beetles drawn to their faint scent of ripening fruit.
Late spring
The great drooping leaves unfold and the zebra swallowtail's first brood lays single eggs on the tender new growth; the tiny fruit clusters begin to set.
Summer
A dense, layered canopy of foot-long leaves shades the ground beneath; zebra swallowtail caterpillars feed in the understory while the green fruit swells.
September
Fruit ripens over a few weeks — skins yellowing and softening, flesh turning to custard — and drops to be found by people, opossums, raccoons, and foxes, usually in that order of speed.
Fall–winter
Leaves turn a clear butter-yellow in October, then fall to reveal smooth grey stems and the fuzzy, dark brown flower buds already waiting for spring.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Pawpaw does not feed the crowd that an oak or a cherry does; its chemistry is too strong for most leaf-eaters. What it offers instead is specialist support. Its flowers are pollinated not by bees but by flies and beetles, its leaves are the sole food of one of Ohio's most striking butterflies, and its fruit is a September feast for the mammals of the river bottoms.

Because the flowers smell faintly of fermenting fruit rather than nectar, bees largely ignore them, and fruit set in the wild is often poor. Some growers hang fish scraps or overripe fruit in their trees at bloom time to call in more flies — an old orchard trick that works. Deer browse around pawpaw and rarely touch it, which is why it spreads in woods where deer have stripped out everything else.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Pawpaw is easy once established and fussy getting there. It grows a long, brittle taproot and resents being moved, so start with a small container-grown tree rather than a dug one, and plant it where it will stay. Young seedlings are burned by full sun and want shade for their first year or two; once established, a pawpaw in more sun flowers and fruits far better than one in deep woods. For fruit, plant at least two genetically different trees — two named cultivars, or seedlings from different sources — because a single clone, or a thicket from one root system, is poorly self-fruitful.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Ohio's Official Native Fruit

In 2009 the Ohio General Assembly named the pawpaw the state's official native fruit, and nowhere celebrates it more than Albany, in Athens County, home of the Ohio Pawpaw Festival each September — pawpaw beer, pawpaw ice cream, a best-fruit contest, and growers comparing cultivars. The fruit bruises in a day and keeps only a few days more, which is why it never became a grocery-store crop and why most people still meet it at a festival or under a tree in the woods.

02

Pollinated by Flies

Pawpaw's maroon, meat-coloured flowers and their faint smell of fermenting fruit are an invitation to flies and beetles, not bees. Those pollinators are unreliable, and each flower's stigmas ripen before its own pollen, so a lone pawpaw — or a whole thicket that is really one plant grown from its roots — often sets little or no fruit. Two trees of different genetic origin, planted close together, change everything, and a patient gardener with a small paintbrush can do the flies' work by hand.

03

The Zebra Swallowtail

The zebra swallowtail, black-and-white striped with long sword-like tails, lays its eggs only on pawpaw, and its range follows the tree along Ohio's river valleys. The caterpillars take in the leaves' acetogenins and keep them into adulthood, which makes the butterflies unpleasant to birds. Plant pawpaw near a stream in southern Ohio and there is a fair chance a zebra swallowtail will find it — they are strong fliers that seek out even small groves.

04

Hoosier Banana

Pawpaw fruit was eaten by Native peoples of the Ohio Valley for thousands of years, noted by the de Soto expedition, planted by Jefferson at Monticello, and carried by the Lewis and Clark party when their other food ran short. Its old names — Hoosier banana, poor man's banana, custard apple — describe the flavour well: banana, mango, and vanilla in a soft yellow custard. The large seeds, the skin, and the leaves are not for eating; enjoy only the ripe flesh.

Native Fruit Trees

Ohio's Native Fruit Trees

Asimina is the only genus of the custard-apple family that reaches beyond the tropics, so pawpaw, profiled above, has no close relatives in Ohio. Its true peers are the other native trees that bear fruit worth eating — each a wildlife tree first, and each a taste of what grew here before the orchard apple.

American Persimmon

Diospyros virginiana

A tree of old fields and dry hillsides in the southern counties, with blocky, alligator-hide bark and orange fruit that is fiercely astringent until fully soft and wrinkled in autumn — then sweet as dates. Male and female trees are separate.

Serviceberry

The first tree of spring to flower in white clouds along the wood edge, followed in June by small blueberry-like fruit that birds and people race to pick. Also called juneberry and shadbush, and a lovely multi-stemmed garden tree.

A thorny, thicket-forming small tree that covers itself in white blossom in April before the leaves, followed by red-purple plums in August for birds, mammals, and people. A fine hedgerow plant and an early nectar source.
