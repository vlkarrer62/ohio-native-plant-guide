About This Plant

A Candelabra Over the Wet Meadow

Blue vervain is the vervain of Ohio's wet places — moist meadows, ditches, stream banks, pond margins, and the low, seasonally flooded ground of floodplains. It is an upright, rather stiff perennial on a distinctly square stem, with pairs of narrow, sharply toothed, lance-shaped leaves, rough to the touch. In July the stem branches at the top into a candelabra of slender, upright spikes, each only a pencil's width across.

The flowers are small and violet-blue, and they open a few at a time in a narrow ring that moves slowly up each spike — green buds above, a band of colour, and spent calyces below. The effect is a fine-textured haze of blue held above the meadow for six weeks or more, and the plant is easy to spot at a distance among grasses and boneset by that branched, upright silhouette.

Blue vervain is a host plant for the common buckeye butterfly and feeds a specialist bee that gathers pollen only from vervains. Its long, ring-by-ring bloom gives bees and butterflies a steady midsummer nectar source in wet ground, and its seeds carry sparrows and juncos into winter.

A Year in the Life

Spring
A rosette of toothed leaves greens up in wet ground; square stems begin to rise by late spring.
Early sum.
Rapid upright growth. The stem branches at the top and the slender spikes form, still green and tightly budded.
Mid–late sum.
Peak bloom from July into September — rings of violet-blue flowers climb each spike, tracked by bees, skippers, and buckeyes.
Fall
Spikes turn brown and hold small nutlets that sparrows and juncos work over as the meadow dries down.
Winter
The stiff candelabra skeleton stands through snow, and dropped seed sows next year's plants.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Because each spike opens only a few flowers a day over many weeks, blue vervain offers a small but dependable daily ration of nectar and pollen through the heart of summer. Short-tongued bees and small butterflies are its main visitors; the flowers are too small for most large butterflies to bother with.

The verbena bee (Calliopsis nebraskensis) is a small ground-nesting specialist that collects pollen only from vervains; planting blue vervain in a wet meadow is one of the few ways to support it. The bitter, rough foliage is rarely browsed by deer or rabbits.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Blue vervain asks for two things: sun and moisture. Give it a wet spot — a rain garden, a ditch line, the downhill edge of a lawn, a pond margin — and it will look after itself. In ordinary garden soil it grows well enough with watering but stays shorter. It is a short-lived perennial that keeps its place by self-sowing, so let a few seed heads stand and expect it to move around a planting over the years.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

A Ring That Climbs the Spike

Look closely at a vervain spike and you will see it is never fully in bloom. The flowers open from the bottom up in a narrow band, just a few at a time, and the band travels slowly toward the tip over several weeks. Above it are unopened green buds; below, the small four-sided calyces left behind. It is a strategy for a long season rather than a big show — and a reliable daily meal for the bees that patrol it.

02

The Bee That Eats Only Vervain

The verbena bee, Calliopsis nebraskensis, is a small, ground-nesting native bee whose females collect pollen exclusively from vervains to provision their nests. Specialists like this one disappear when their host plants do; putting blue vervain into a wet meadow restores a link that a lawn or a ditch of reed canary grass cannot.

03

Simpler's Joy

One of the plant's old names is simpler's joy — a "simpler" being a gatherer of medicinal herbs, or simples. The name and reputation were inherited from the European vervain, Verbena officinalis, a plant of great ceremonial importance to the Romans and Druids, and transferred by settlers to its blue-flowered American cousin. It is mentioned here as history, not as a use.

04

The Buckeye's Nursery

The common buckeye — the brown butterfly with bold eyespots that arrives in Ohio each summer from the south — lays its eggs on vervains among a few other plants, and its spiny black caterpillars feed on the leaves. Blue vervain also hosts the verbena moth. Neither does lasting harm to an established colony.

Genus Verbena

Meet the Vervains

Verbena is a mostly American genus of square-stemmed plants with small flowers on narrow spikes. Ohio has several native species, and they sort themselves neatly by moisture: blue vervain, profiled above, is the one of wet ground; its relatives take the dry prairies, the woodland edges, and the gravelly waste places.
