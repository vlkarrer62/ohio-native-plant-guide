About This Plant

Oats on One Side

Sideoats grama is the grass of the dry prairie — a tidy, upright bunchgrass one to three feet tall with narrow, slightly blue-green leaves, at home on thin limestone soils, gravelly slopes, and sun-baked ground where taller grasses struggle. It is one of the dominant grasses of the Great Plains, and reaches Ohio at the eastern edge of its range, in scattered dry prairie remnants and open limestone glades.

The name describes it exactly. From midsummer the slender flower stems carry a row of thirty to sixty small, oat-like spikelets, and every one of them hangs from the same side of the stem, like flags along a line. When they open, each dangles a set of bright orange-red anthers and feathery purple stigmas — for a grass, an unusually showy bloom. In fall the whole plant turns tan and bronze, and the spikelets drop as units, one by one.

Sideoats grama is a host plant for grass skippers — the small orange butterflies that dart across every meadow — and its seed feeds sparrows, juncos, and turkeys into winter. As a compact, drought-proof matrix grass it knits a dry planting together without swamping the flowers.

A Year in the Life

Spring
Slow to wake, like all warm-season grasses; new blue-green leaves appear in May as the soil warms, low among the spring flowers.
Early sum.
The tuft fills out and the flower stems rise; still a neat, low mound that lets the prairie flowers show.
Mid–late sum.
Peak bloom from July into September — one-sided rows of spikelets on every stem, hung with orange anthers that glow with the sun behind them.
Fall
Foliage turns tan, bronze, and purplish; the ripe spikelets feed sparrows and drop one by one as the plant dries.
Winter
The straw-coloured tuft and bare stems stand through winter, holding seed and sheltering insects until cut back in spring.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Grasses are wind-pollinated, so no bees come for the flowers — sideoats grama serves wildlife as food, host, and cover. Its leaves raise the caterpillars of several grass skippers, its seed feeds ground-foraging birds, and its dense tufts are where grasshoppers, meadow katydids, and beetles hide from the birds that eat them.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Sideoats grama wants sun, drainage, and not much else. It is happiest on lean, dry, even limy ground where rich soil and irrigation would make it flop and let weeds in. It is slow the first year, as warm-season grasses are, and then settles into a long-lived, drought-proof clump that needs cutting once a year and nothing more. In Ohio it can seed around gently on open ground.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Why the Oats Hang One Way

Each flower stem carries dozens of short branches, and every branch twists at its base so that it hangs down the same side of the stem — the "side oats." When the seed is ripe the branches fall off whole, spikelet and all, and the stem is left bare and zigzagged. The species name curtipendula means "short-hanging," and the arrangement is so distinctive that the grass can be named from across a field in September.

02

A Grass That Flowers Like a Flower

Most grasses bloom invisibly. Sideoats grama hangs out bright orange anthers — the pollen-bearing parts — that dangle from every open spikelet like tiny lanterns, with purple, feathery stigmas beside them to catch pollen on the wind. Backlit on a July morning, a stand of it glows. It is one of the few native grasses that earns a place in a border for its flowers alone.

03

The Plains Come to Ohio

Sideoats grama is a signature grass of the mixed-grass prairie of Kansas, Nebraska, and the Dakotas, and the state grass of Texas. Its range thins eastward, and in Ohio it survives as a rarity on dry limestone prairies and glades — the same thin-soiled openings that hold the state's other western holdouts. Planting it in a dry garden here is a small act of restoration for a plant at the edge of its world.

04

The Grama Grasses

"Grama" comes from the Spanish word for grass, and the gramas — genus Bouteloua, named for two Spanish botanist brothers — are the grasses that fed the bison herds. Sideoats is the tallest and most eastern of them; its relatives, blue grama and buffalograss, are the short, tough, sod-forming grasses of the shortgrass plains that are now being planted as low-water lawns. The genus is the subject of the showcase below.

Genus Bouteloua

Meet the Gramas

Bouteloua — the grama grasses — are the drought-hardy warm-season grasses of the western plains, most of them short and sod-forming. Sideoats grama, profiled above, is the one that reaches Ohio; its relatives are worth knowing because they are the grasses behind the new wave of low-water native lawns.

The grass of the bison range — a soft, grey-green, stoloniferous turf a few inches high that needs almost no water or mowing. Recently moved into Bouteloua from its own genus. A plains species, not native this far east.

Hairy Grama

Bouteloua hirsuta

A small, tufted grama of sandy and rocky ground with one or two curved, bristly seed heads per stem, each tipped with a tiny point. Rare in the Midwest, scattered on dunes and glades.
