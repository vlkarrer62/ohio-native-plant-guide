About This Plant

Honey-Balls at the Water's Edge

Buttonbush is the shrub of the shoreline — of pond margins, swamp edges, oxbows, and river banks, where it stands with its roots in the water for weeks at a time and thrives. It is a loose, rounded, multi-stemmed shrub six to twelve feet high with glossy, pointed leaves in pairs or whorls of three, late to leaf out in spring and among the last shrubs to look alive in May.

Then, from late June into August, it produces the flowers that make it unforgettable: perfect white spheres an inch or more across, each made of two hundred tiny tubular florets packed so evenly that the ball is geometric, and bristling all over with long, protruding styles like a pincushion. They are sweetly fragrant and full of nectar, and on a July afternoon a buttonbush in bloom is covered with more butterflies than almost any other native shrub. Round, brown seed balls follow and hang on the bare twigs through winter.

Buttonbush is the best nectar shrub for wet ground in Ohio — swallowtails, skippers, monarchs, bees, and hummingbirds all work the flower balls — and its seed heads feed ducks and shorebirds into winter. It is also one of very few shrubs that will grow in standing water, holding pond edges and rain-garden basins that drown everything else.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
Slow to wake — bare twigs into May while everything around it is green, then glossy new leaves unfold quickly once the water warms.
Early–mid sum.
Peak bloom from late June through July — fragrant white flower balls at every branch tip, crowded with butterflies and bees from morning to dusk.
Late summer
A few late balls open into August as the first ones turn to green, then reddish, seed heads; sphinx moth caterpillars feed on the leaves.
Fall
Leaves turn a modest yellow and drop; the round brown seed balls ripen and begin to break apart, feeding wood ducks and mallards on the water below.
Winter
Bare, angular stems hold the last seed balls over the ice, a distinctive winter silhouette along the shore.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Buttonbush is a nectar plant first. Each ball holds hundreds of small tubular flowers with nectar at the base, and their sheer number and open access suit almost every visitor — large butterflies that perch on the sphere, bees that work it round, and hummingbirds that hover at it. The seed balls and dense, water-standing thickets serve a second set of animals entirely.

The seed balls break into small nutlets that float, and ducks and shorebirds gather them from the water surface in fall and winter. Thickets standing in water are nesting cover for red-winged blackbirds and loafing cover for wood ducks. The foliage is bitter and not browsed by deer.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Buttonbush asks for water and sun. In its natural setting it grows in soil that is saturated or flooded much of the year; in a garden it is content in any reliably moist bed, and it is the one shrub to plant in the bottom of a rain-garden basin or at a pond edge where the ground is sometimes under water. It will survive in ordinary soil with watering, but never looks its best there. It is fast, tough, and takes hard pruning without complaint.

LightFull sun for the heaviest bloom; tolerates part shade
SoilAny, including heavy clay and muck; tolerates standing water to a depth of a foot or more

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Perfect Spheres

A buttonbush flower head is not a flower but a crowd of them — around two hundred small white tubes arranged on a round core so evenly that the head is a near-perfect sphere. Each floret pushes its style out past the petals before its own pollen is ripe, so the ball bristles with them, and a visiting butterfly walking over the surface pollinates dozens of flowers at once. The name is old and obvious: the heads look like the cloth-covered buttons of a nineteenth-century coat.

02

The Shrub That Grows in the Pond

Most woody plants drown when their roots are flooded for more than a few days. Buttonbush is one of the rare exceptions: its roots and stems tolerate weeks of standing water, and in swamps and lake margins it grows in water a foot or two deep, where it forms the "buttonbush swamp" community that ecologists name after it. That is what makes it the answer to the wettest corner of a yard — the spot where the downspouts drain and the ground never dries.

03

Honey-Balls

Beekeepers have an old name for buttonbush — honey-balls — because the nectar flow from a swamp full of it in July is heavy enough to make a distinct, pale honey. The same nectar is why it out-draws almost every other shrub for butterflies: it flowers in the midsummer gap after the spring shrubs and before the goldenrods, when large butterflies are at their peak and hunting for a big, reliable source.

04

A Relative of Coffee

Buttonbush belongs to the Rubiaceae, the coffee family — the family of coffee, gardenia, and quinine, almost all of them tropical. It is one of only a handful of that family that reach the northern United States as woody plants, and its round heads of small, four-lobed, tubular flowers are the family signature. Its tiny Ohio cousins are the bluets and partridgeberry of the woodland floor. As with many of its relatives, the foliage contains bitter compounds that make it toxic to livestock and unpalatable to deer.

Shrubs of the Water's Edge

Ohio's shorelines and swamp edges have a shrub layer of their own. Buttonbush, profiled above, is the one that goes deepest into the water and draws the most butterflies; these three companions carry the same wet shrub layer through bird fruit, summer roses, and winter berries.

Elderberry

Sambucus canadensis

The fast, suckering shrub of ditch banks and wet edges with flat white flower plates in June and purple-black berries in August for more than forty kinds of birds. Tolerates wet feet but not open water.

Swamp Rose

Rosa palustris

A native rose of marsh and pond edges with arching, thorny canes, fragrant single pink flowers in June and July, and red hips that persist into winter for birds. Grows in saturated soil beside buttonbush.
