About This Plant

The Wild Hydrangea of the Ravines

Long before there were mophead hydrangeas in every suburban yard, there was this one, growing on the shaded rocky slopes, ravine walls, and stream banks of the eastern woods. Wild hydrangea is a low, rounded, suckering shrub three to five feet high with big, opposite, heart-shaped, toothed leaves and thin stems whose bark peels in papery layers — the source of its old name, sevenbark.

From June into August it carries flat-topped flower heads up to six inches across. Look closely and each head is two kinds of flower: a dome of hundreds of tiny, cream-coloured fertile flowers full of pollen and nectar, ringed by a scattering of large, four-petaled, sterile white florets whose only job is to advertise. That lacecap arrangement is the wild plant's design, and it is why the wild species feeds insects that its famous all-sterile descendant cannot.

Wild hydrangea is the shrub for rocky, rooty shade — the slope under the trees where little else will grow — and a midsummer pollen and nectar source for bumblebees, small bees, hover flies, and beetles in the shaded garden, when the woodland flowers have long finished. Its suckering roots hold banks together.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
New shoots rise from the base and from last year's stems; the big leaves unfold in May and the shrub fills out quickly into a green mound.
Early–mid sum.
Peak bloom from June into July — flat cream lacecaps on every shoot, worked by bumblebees, small bees, and hover flies through the shaded garden.
Late summer
A few late heads keep opening into August; the early ones turn green, then tan, and hold their shape as dried heads.
Fall
Leaves turn a soft yellow and drop; the tan flower heads persist on the bare stems.
Winter
Thin, pale, peeling stems and dried heads through winter; cut back in late winter, since the plant flowers on new wood.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

The fertile flowers at the centre of each lacecap are small, open, and abundant, which suits a broad crowd of short-tongued insects — the same bees, flies, and beetles that work elderberry and the mountain mints. Because it blooms in shade in midsummer, it fills a gap that few other native shrubs cover.

The dense, low mound is cover for ground-feeding birds and small mammals on a slope, and the suckering roots hold soil. Note that deer do browse wild hydrangea, especially young shoots in spring, so in heavy-browse areas it wants protection until established.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Wild hydrangea is among the easiest native shrubs for shade. It wants the conditions of a wooded slope — shade or dappled light, loose well-drained soil, and moisture in spring — and once established it tolerates drier, rootier ground than most shade shrubs. Because it flowers on the current year's growth, it can be cut back hard every late winter and will bloom the same summer, which keeps it compact and full. It suckers slowly into a colony.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

The Fertile and the Showy

A wild hydrangea head is a division of labour. The hundreds of tiny fertile flowers in the middle make the pollen and nectar and set the seed; the few big white florets around the edge are sterile — no stamens, no nectar — and exist only to catch an insect's eye from a distance. Breeders found that if you select for more and more sterile florets you get a snowball of white and nothing for the bees to eat. That is the whole difference between the wild plant and the mophead in the front yard.

02

Annabelle's Story

The most-planted native shrub in America is a wild hydrangea. In 1910, near the town of Anna, Illinois, a woman named Harriet Kirkpatrick noticed a wild hydrangea on a wooded hillside whose every floret was sterile, so that the heads were huge white balls. She dug it up and planted it in town, and gardeners passed cuttings around for fifty years before a horticulturist at the University of Illinois named it 'Annabelle' and released it in 1960. Every Annabelle in the world is a piece of that one plant.

03

Sevenbark

Scratch an older stem and you find bark that peels away in thin, papery layers of different colours — tan, cinnamon, grey — one beneath another. Settlers counted seven and named the shrub sevenbark. The root was used in Cherokee and later settler medicine, and the plant was sold by nineteenth-century herbalists under that name; it is mentioned here as background only. The peeling stems are a good winter identification mark on a leafless slope.

04

A Shrub for the Slope Nothing Wants

Wild hydrangea's natural home is the steep, rocky, north-facing bank above a stream — thin soil, tree roots, shade, and a spring flush of moisture that dries out by August. Those are exactly the conditions of the difficult corner in most shaded yards: the slope under the maples, the strip along the north foundation. It is one of the few native shrubs that not only survives there but flowers, and its suckering roots knit the bank together as it spreads.

Shrubs for Rocky Shade

Dry, rooty, rocky shade is the hardest ground in a garden, and Ohio's ravines have a set of native shrubs that were made for it. Wild hydrangea, profiled above, is the best for midsummer flowers; these three companions add spring bloom, fruit, and structure to the same slope.

Ninebark

Physocarpus opulifolius

A tough, arching shrub of rocky stream banks and bluffs with white flower clusters in late spring, reddish seed capsules, and bark that peels in many layers. Takes more sun and drier ground than hydrangea, and is nearly indestructible.
