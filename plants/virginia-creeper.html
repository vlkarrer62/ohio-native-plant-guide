About This Plant

Leaves of Five

Virginia creeper is the native vine everyone in Ohio has seen and few have planted. It runs along fencerows and up tree trunks, over stone walls and across the ground at the edge of every wood, a vigorous woody climber that can reach fifty feet into a canopy. Its leaves are unmistakable once known: five toothed leaflets spread like the fingers of a hand, on a long stalk. It climbs not by twining but by branched tendrils tipped with tiny adhesive discs that glue it to bark or masonry, so it needs no support to scale a wall.

The flowers are small and greenish and easy to miss in June, but what follows is not. In late August the leaves begin turning — the earliest and most brilliant scarlet of any native plant — and beneath them hang clusters of blue-black berries on bright red stalks. The colour is a signal: the vine is flagging its fruit to the migrating birds that will strip it within weeks. It belongs to the grape family, and its berries, unlike a grape's, are not for people.

Virginia creeper is one of the great bird-fruit plants of the eastern woods — thrushes, woodpeckers, warblers, bluebirds, and more than thirty other species eat the berries — and a host plant for five sphinx moths and the eight-spotted forester. Its scarlet leaves in September are a fruit advertisement timed to the fall migration.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
Reddish new leaves unfold along the old stems in late April and turn green; the vine grows several feet a week and sends out new tendrils.
Summer
Small greenish flowers in June and July, visited by small bees and flies; the leaves are a dense green curtain that hides nests and feeds sphinx moth caterpillars.
Late summer
The first leaves turn scarlet in late August, weeks before other plants, as the berries ripen from green to blue-black on red stalks.
Fall
Peak colour in September and early October — whole trees and fences draped in crimson — while migrating thrushes, warblers, and woodpeckers strip the fruit.
Winter
Bare, grey, disc-footed stems trace the bark; any berries the migrants missed feed winter woodpeckers and chickadees.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Virginia creeper is a bird plant and a moth plant. The berries ripen at the peak of fall migration and are a major fuel for thrushes and warblers moving south; the leaves are the food of an unusual number of large, handsome sphinx moth caterpillars; and the dense curtain of foliage on a fence or trunk is nesting and roosting cover through summer.

The sphinx moths are the surprise: their finger-sized caterpillars, some with the horn at the tail and some with eyespots, feed on creeper leaves in late summer and turn into big, fast-flying moths that hover at evening flowers like hummingbirds. The berries are mildly toxic to people and should be left to the birds. Deer browse the foliage lightly.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Virginia creeper grows almost anywhere — sun or deep shade, wet or dry, clay or sand — and the only question is where you want it. Given a fence, a wall, a dead tree, or a slope, it covers it in two or three seasons and stays covered. Its adhesive discs leave marks on paint and can work into old mortar, so keep it off wood siding and crumbling brick, and keep it out of small trees it could smother. On a chain-link fence, a bare bank, or a mature tree trunk, it is nearly perfect.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Leaves of Three, Leaves of Five

Virginia creeper is the plant most often mistaken for poison ivy, and the two do grow side by side up the same trunks. The rule is in the rhyme: leaves of three, let it be; leaves of five, let it thrive. Poison ivy has three leaflets and climbs by hairy aerial roots; creeper has five and climbs by tendrils. Creeper's sap can irritate sensitive skin, and the berries are poisonous to eat, but it carries none of poison ivy's oil and can be handled freely.

02

The Red Flag

Why does Virginia creeper turn scarlet in late August, weeks before the maples? Ecologists call it foliar fruit flagging: the berries ripen exactly when thrushes and warblers are moving south, and a blaze of red on an otherwise green wood edge tells a passing flock where the food is. The birds eat the fruit, carry the seeds miles, and plant the next fencerow. The colour is not for us; it is a billboard aimed at migrants.

03

Feet That Glue

The vine's tendrils end in small pads that swell on contact and cement themselves to whatever they touch, so the plant can climb a smooth wall no twining vine could grip. Charles Darwin studied them in the 1860s for his book on climbing plants and found that a single disc could hold several ounces, and a whole stem a considerable weight. It is why the vine can scale a stone church, and why it should be kept off anything with paint.

04

The Sphinx Moths' Vine

Turn over creeper leaves in August and you may find a caterpillar as long as a finger, green or brown, with a row of eyespots along its sides or a curved horn at its tail. These are the sphinx moths — the Virginia creeper sphinx, the pandorus, Abbott's, the achemon — whose adults are the big, streamlined moths that hover at evening primroses and bergamot at dusk. Few plants in Ohio raise as many of them, and none is easier to grow.

Vines for Ohio Gardens

Native vines are the least-used layer in the wildlife garden, and Ohio has good ones for every purpose. Virginia creeper, profiled above, is the toughest and the best for birds; these three companions bring hummingbirds, winter fruit, and a swallowtail to fences, arbors, and edges.

Lonicera sempervirens

A well-behaved twining vine with clusters of coral-red tubular flowers from May to frost, built for the ruby-throated hummingbird, and red berries for birds. Nothing like the invasive Japanese honeysuckle; ideal on a trellis or fence.

American Bittersweet

Celastrus scandens

A twining vine of wood edges with orange capsules that split to show scarlet seeds in October and hang on into winter for birds. Plant only the native — the invasive Oriental bittersweet looks alike and strangles trees. Needs a male and female.

Dutchman's Pipe

Isotrema macrophyllum

A big-leaved Appalachian vine with curious pipe-shaped flowers, the host plant of the pipevine swallowtail — the toxic model that the dark tiger swallowtail female mimics. Dense enough for a porch screen, and native to the hills south of Ohio.
