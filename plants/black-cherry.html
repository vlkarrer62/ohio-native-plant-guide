About This Plant

The Fencerow Cherry

Black cherry is the largest native cherry in North America and one of the commonest trees in Ohio, because birds plant it: every fencerow, roadside, and abandoned field grows a line of them from seeds dropped by robins and waxwings. In the open it is a spreading tree of fifty feet; in old woods it can be a straight-trunked eighty-foot timber tree. The bark is the identification mark — smooth and marked with horizontal lenticels when young, then breaking into dark, scaly plates that foresters compare to burnt potato chips.

The leaves are glossy, narrow, and finely toothed, with a line of rusty hairs along the midrib beneath, and they smell of bitter almond when crushed. In May, after the leaves are out, the tree hangs itself with drooping racemes of small white flowers, and in August the racemes carry cherries that ripen from red to purple-black — small, bittersweet, and taken by birds and mammals within days of ripening. The wood beneath the rough bark is the prized reddish cabinet wood of American furniture.

In the eastern states the cherries and plums support more than four hundred species of caterpillar — second only to the oaks — including the eastern tiger swallowtail, red-spotted purple, and cecropia moth. Add fruit for fifty kinds of birds and spring flowers for bees, and black cherry is one of the most productive wildlife trees in Ohio.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
Glossy leaves unfold in April, often with the silk tents of eastern tent caterpillars in the branch forks — a nuisance to the eye and a feast for cuckoos.
Late spring
Peak bloom in May — drooping white racemes on every branch, fragrant and busy with bees, flies, and the first swallowtails laying eggs on the new leaves.
Summer
Hundreds of caterpillar species feed in the canopy, and the birds that nest there feed on them; the green cherries swell along the racemes.
Late summer
Cherries ripen from red to black in August and September and are stripped by robins, waxwings, thrushes, and grosbeaks in a matter of days.
Fall–winter
Leaves turn yellow and drop early; the dark scaly bark and the fine, dark twigs with their almond scent identify the tree through winter.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Black cherry works for wildlife at three seasons. Its May flowers are a broad, open nectar and pollen source for bees and flies at the end of the spring bloom. Its leaves, through summer, raise one of the largest caterpillar communities of any native tree, which is what feeds nesting songbirds. And its fruit in late summer is taken by an enormous range of birds and mammals.

The yellow-billed cuckoo is a specialist of black cherry's tent caterpillars, one of the few birds that eats the hairy larvae, and cuckoo numbers track caterpillar years. The wilted leaves release cyanide and are dangerous to livestock, which is why farmers clear it from pasture edges; in a wildlife garden it is one of the best trees there is.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Black cherry needs almost no help — birds plant it everywhere, it grows fast in sun, and it tolerates most soils. The practical questions are where. It drops fruit that stains, it seeds into beds, its wilted leaves can poison grazing animals, and its wood is brittle in storms, so it belongs at the edge of a property or in a wildlife planting rather than over a patio. Given that, a black cherry at the back of a yard is a decade from being a full-grown wildlife tree.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Four Hundred Caterpillars

When entomologists counted the caterpillar species that eat each kind of native tree, the oaks came first and the cherries and plums came second, with well over four hundred in the Mid-Atlantic states. Caterpillars are what songbirds feed their young — a single chickadee brood takes thousands — so a black cherry in leaf is, in effect, a bird feeder that runs from May to September. It is the reason a scruffy fencerow cherry is worth more to wildlife than most ornamental trees put together.

02

Burnt Potato Chips

A young black cherry has smooth, dark, reddish bark banded with pale horizontal lenticels, much like a birch's; an old one is covered in small, dark, curling scales that every forestry student learns to call burnt potato chips. Scratch a twig and it smells strongly of bitter almond — the cyanide-releasing compounds that make the wilted leaves dangerous to cattle and horses, and that make the cherry pits, as with all cherries, something not to chew.

03

Rum Cherry and Cabinet Wood

Settlers steeped the bittersweet fruit in rum and brandy, which gave the tree its old name of rum cherry, and the bark went into cough syrups for a century — mentioned here as history, not as advice. The wood is the real prize: fine-grained, warm red, and darkening with age, black cherry is the most valuable furniture hardwood of the eastern forest, and old-growth cherry from the Allegheny Plateau is sought after by cabinetmakers around the world.

04

The Tents in the Forks

In April, before most trees have leaves, black cherries sprout silk tents in their branch forks: the nests of the eastern tent caterpillar, which hatches early and shelters in the web between feeding trips. The tents look alarming and do the tree no lasting harm — it releafs by June — and they are the reason the yellow-billed cuckoo, one of the few birds that will eat hairy caterpillars, nests where black cherries grow. Leave the tents; the cuckoos are counting on them.

Genus Prunus

Meet the Native Cherries & Plums

Prunus — the cherries and plums — is the second most important genus for caterpillars in the eastern states, and Ohio has several natives beyond black cherry, profiled above. Its relatives are smaller trees and shrubs of edges and thickets, each with spring flowers and fruit for birds.

Chokecherry

Prunus virginiana

A suckering shrub or small tree of edges and stream banks with similar white racemes and dark red cherries so astringent they pucker the mouth — hence the name — but beloved by birds. The shrub-sized cherry for a hedge.

American Plum

Prunus americana

A thorny, thicket-forming small tree that covers itself in white blossom in April before the leaves, followed by red-purple plums in August for birds, mammals, and people. A fine hedgerow plant and an early nectar source.

Pin Cherry

Prunus pensylvanica

A short-lived pioneer of clearings and burns in the north and east of the state, with shiny red bark, flowers in small clusters rather than racemes, and bright red cherries in July that birds take at once. Rarely planted, but a quick wildlife tree.
