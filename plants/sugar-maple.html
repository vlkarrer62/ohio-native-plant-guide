About This Plant

The Maple of the Deep Woods

Sugar maple is the great tree of Ohio's rich, moist forests — the beech-maple woods of the glaciated north and the ravines and north-facing slopes of the south. It is among the most shade-tolerant trees in the state: its seedlings carpet the forest floor and can wait for decades in the gloom beneath their parents, growing a few inches a year, until a gap in the canopy opens and they race upward. In the open it becomes a broad, dense, oval tree of sixty to eighty feet; in the forest, a tall, clean trunk with grey bark that breaks into long, firm, upturned plates.

The leaves are the maple leaf of the Canadian flag — five-lobed, with a few large, blunt teeth and smooth, U-shaped sinuses between the lobes, set in opposite pairs on long stalks. In April, as the leaves unfold, the tree hangs out tassels of tiny yellow-green flowers on threadlike stalks, visited by early bees and carried off by the wind. By fall they have become paired, U-shaped samaras that spin to the ground, and the leaves turn the famous yellow, orange, and scarlet of an Ohio October.

The maples host nearly three hundred species of caterpillar in the eastern states — among them the rosy maple moth, the cecropia, and the maple spanworm — and they are one of the canopy trees that feed nesting songbirds through the summer. Add April pollen for early bees, seed for squirrels and finches, and the deep shade that shelters a woodland wildflower floor, and sugar maple is a keystone of Ohio's forests.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Late winter
Freezing nights and thawing days set the sap running in February and March — the sugaring season, when Ohio's sugarbushes are tapped and the sugarhouses steam.
Spring
In April the tree hangs out tassels of yellow-green flowers on long, drooping stalks as the pale new leaves unfold, and early bumblebees and sweat bees work them for pollen.
Summer
A dense canopy of deep green, casting the heaviest shade of any Ohio tree, with rosy maple moth and hundreds of other caterpillars feeding the songbirds nesting above.
Fall
In October the leaves turn yellow, orange, and scarlet — often all three on one tree — and the paired samaras ripen and spin down for squirrels, chipmunks, and finches.
Winter
Grey, platy bark, opposite branching, and sharp, brown, cone-shaped buds identify the bare tree; the fallen leaves lie as a rich, fast-rotting mulch.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Sugar maple gives in three ways. Its April flowers, open to both the wind and insects, are an early pollen and nectar source for bees coming out of winter. Its leaves, all summer, raise one of the larger caterpillar communities among native trees — food for the warblers, vireos, and grosbeaks that nest in its canopy. And its seeds, bark, and hollows feed and shelter mammals and birds through the year.

The rosy maple moth — a small, woolly moth in raspberry pink and butter yellow — is the maples' signature caterpillar, and its green-striped larvae feed in groups on the leaves in summer. Yellow-bellied sapsuckers drill neat rows of wells in the bark in spring and return to lap the sweet sap, and hummingbirds and early insects visit the wells after them.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Sugar maple is a forest tree, and it grows best where the forest would: deep, cool, moist, well-drained soil with its own leaf litter left beneath it. Give it that and it is long-lived, strong-wooded, and nearly trouble-free. It does poorly as a street tree — road salt, compacted soil, reflected heat, and drought all stress it, and scorched leaf edges in August are the sign. Plant it in a lawn, park, or woodland edge with room to spread, mulch the root zone widely, and water through its first dry summers.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Forty Gallons to One

Sugar maple sap runs clear and watery, only about two percent sugar, so it takes roughly forty gallons of it, boiled down for hours, to make a single gallon of syrup. It flows best on the swing between freezing nights and thawing days in late winter, when pressure inside the trunk pushes sap out of a tap hole. Ohio is a real maple-syrup state: Indigenous peoples made maple sugar here long before settlers, and today hundreds of sugarbushes — thickest in the northeast, where Geauga County holds a maple festival every spring — still tap the trees each March.

02

Where the Colors Come From

The yellow and orange of a maple in October were in the leaf all summer: carotenoid pigments, hidden under green chlorophyll until the tree stops making it in fall. The scarlet is new. As the days shorten, a sugar maple traps sugars in its leaves and builds red anthocyanins from them — most of all after sunny days and cool, frost-free nights. That is why a bright, crisp October brings a brilliant fall, and why one tree can be gold on its shaded side and scarlet on its sunny one.

03

Watering Its Neighbors

On summer nights, when its leaves close their pores, a sugar maple draws water up from moist soil deep below and releases it through its shallow roots into the dry topsoil — then drinks it back up again the next morning. This hydraulic lift, first measured in sugar maples, can supply a real share of the tree's daily water, and some of that moisture is taken up by the shallow-rooted wildflowers and shrubs growing beneath it.

04

Hard Maple and Bird's-Eye

Lumbermen call it hard maple or rock maple, and the name is earned: the pale, dense, fine-grained wood is tough enough for bowling alleys, basketball courts, butcher blocks, and baseball bats. Now and then a tree grows wood full of tiny swirling knots, like scattered eyes — bird's-eye maple, whose cause is still debated and which is prized by cabinetmakers and luthiers. Curly, or "tiger," maple, with its rippled shimmer, comes from the same tree.

Genus Acer

Meet Ohio's Native Maples

Acer — the maples — is one of the most important tree genera for caterpillars in the eastern states, and Ohio has several natives beyond sugar maple, profiled above. All share opposite leaves and paired, winged samaras; they differ in leaf shape, habitat, and how early they flower.

Red Maple

Acer rubrum

The most widespread maple in the East, at home from swamps to dry ridges, with three-lobed, sharply toothed leaves and clusters of red flowers in March — one of the first bee flowers of the year. Reliable scarlet fall color.

Silver Maple

Acer saccharinum

A big, fast, riverbank tree with deeply cut leaves that flash silver-white beneath, flowering in late winter and dropping large samaras by May. Brittle and aggressive-rooted near buildings, but a fine tree for floodplains.

Black Maple

Acer nigrum

So close to sugar maple that many botanists treat it as a subspecies: darker bark, leaves that droop at the edges and are usually three-lobed and velvety beneath. Found in moist bottomlands and tapped for syrup just the same.

Boxelder

Acer negundo

The maple that doesn't look like one: leaves divided into three to five leaflets, green twigs, and male and female flowers on separate trees. A quick, weedy stream-bank tree, host to the boxelder bug and a winter seed source for evening grosbeaks.
