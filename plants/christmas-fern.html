About This Plant

Green at Christmas

Christmas fern is the fern you find in every Ohio woodland — on shaded slopes and ravine sides, along stream banks, under oaks and beeches, often the only green thing on the forest floor in January. It grows as a vase-shaped clump of leathery, dark green fronds one to two feet long, rising from a central crown that sits at the soil surface. Each frond is divided once into thirty or more narrow leaflets, and every leaflet has a small lobe pointing up at its base, so that it is shaped, unmistakably, like a Christmas stocking.

The fronds are evergreen. They stand upright through the growing season, then lie flat on the ground in late fall and stay green under the snow until the new fiddleheads — silvery with scales — uncurl through them in April. In early summer some fronds send up narrowed, spore-bearing tips, brown with spore cases beneath; the rest stay sterile and glossy. A fern has no flowers, so its wildlife value is all in what it shelters and holds.

Christmas fern is the evergreen structure of the shade garden — a year-round clump that holds slopes against erosion, keeps the ground beneath it cool and damp, and gives ground beetles, salamanders, and overwintering insects a roof in winter. It does this in dry shade under trees where almost nothing else will grow.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
Fiddleheads covered in silvery-brown scales push up through last year's flattened fronds in April and uncurl over two or three weeks — the fern's one moment of drama.
Late spring
New fronds harden to a glossy dark green and stand upright in a vase; the old fronds wither away beneath them.
Summer
Fertile fronds raise narrowed tips crowded with brown spore cases on the underside; spores drift on the wind from June to August.
Fall
As the leaves come down the fronds stay green, and in late fall they relax and lie flat on the litter, radiating from the crown.
Winter
Evergreen under snow — the deep green rosettes on a brown hillside are the season the fern is named for, and a roof for everything wintering beneath.

Wildlife Support

Who Shelters Here

A fern makes no nectar, pollen, seed, or fruit, so its contribution is habitat. Christmas fern's dense, evergreen clumps are the winter cover of the woodland floor: a dry, insulated space beneath the flattened fronds where ground beetles, spiders, and firefly larvae overwinter, salamanders and toads shelter, and ground-nesting birds tuck their nests in spring. A few moth caterpillars feed on the fronds.

The fern's value is greatest in winter, when its flattened fronds insulate the soil and litter beneath them. It is left alone by deer and rabbits, which makes it one of the few evergreen shade plants that survives heavy browse pressure intact.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Christmas fern is the toughest of Ohio's woodland ferns. It wants shade and a loose, leafy soil, but unlike most ferns it tolerates dry shade once established, holds on slopes, and shrugs off drought that browns the others. The crown must sit at the soil surface — buried, it rots — and the plant should be left undisturbed; it is slow to spread and can live for decades in one place. Plant it in groups for the vase shapes to read, and leave the old fronds in place until the new ones are up.

LightPart shade to full shade; tolerates morning sun; scorches in afternoon sun
SoilLoose, humusy, well-drained; acidic to neutral; tolerates rocky ground and slopes
MoistureMedium to moist preferred; tolerates dry shade once established, which few ferns do
HabitSlow-growing clump from a single crown, 1–2 ft tall and wide; does not run
PlantingSet the crown at soil level, never below; space 18–24 in apart
MaintenanceNone; remove flattened old fronds in spring if wanted, after the fiddleheads rise
PropagationDivide crowns with several growing points in early spring; spores are slow

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

The Stocking on Every Leaflet

Look at a single leaflet and it has a small, rounded lobe pointing up from its base, like the toe of a stocking hung from the frond — the feature that identifies the fern at a glance and that gave it, with its evergreen habit, the name Christmas fern. Early settlers in the Ohio Valley cut the green fronds in December for wreaths and church decoration, one of the few fresh greens available in the winter woods.

02

Two Kinds of Frond

Not every frond on a Christmas fern is the same. Most are sterile — glossy, broad, and evergreen. A few each summer are fertile: their upper leaflets shrink to half size and turn brown beneath with dense patches of spore cases, so the frond looks as if its tip has withered. The species name acrostichoides refers to the way those spore cases cover the whole underside of the leaflet. Sterile fronds do the year-round work; fertile fronds do the reproducing and die back first.

03

Ferns Before Flowers

Ferns are far older than flowering plants — their ancestors formed the coal forests three hundred million years ago — and they reproduce without flowers, pollen, or seed. A spore that lands on damp ground grows into a tiny heart-shaped plant a few millimetres across, which produces eggs and swimming sperm; only after fertilisation does a new fern rise from it. That is why ferns need moisture to spread, and why a woodland garden gains them slowly, by division, rather than by seed.

04

Flat for the Winter

The fern's most useful habit is the one that looks like collapse. In late fall the fronds relax and lie flat on the ground in a green star around the crown. It is not damage: pressed to the litter, the fronds are protected from wind and ice, keep photosynthesising on mild winter days, and make an insulated roof over the soil where firefly larvae, beetles, and salamanders wait out the cold. In April the new fiddleheads rise straight through them.

Native Ferns

Ferns for Ohio Shade

Ohio has some fifty native ferns, and a handful are garden staples for shade. Christmas fern, profiled above, is the evergreen, the drought-tolerant one, and the one to start with; these three companions bring different textures and moisture needs to the same woodland floor.

Most Common · Evergreen · Dry Shade

Christmas Fern

Polystichum acrostichoides

The species profiled on this page: leathery evergreen fronds in a vase, stocking-shaped leaflets, and a tolerance for dry, rooty shade and slopes no other Ohio fern matches. Clumping, long-lived, deer-proof.

Maidenhair Fern

Adiantum pedatum

The most delicate native fern — a flat, fan-shaped whorl of fine leaflets on glossy black wiry stems, in rich, moist, shaded ravines. Deciduous, slow-spreading, and unlike anything else on the forest floor.

Ostrich Fern

Matteuccia struthiopteris

A big, plume-like fern of floodplains and stream banks, three to five feet tall, spreading by runners into colonies. The source of the edible spring fiddleheads. Needs moist soil and room; too vigorous for a small bed.

Sensitive Fern

Onoclea sensibilis

A coarse, broad-leaved fern of wet meadows and ditch edges that spreads by runners and dies to the ground at the first frost — hence "sensitive." Its stiff, bead-like fertile fronds stand all winter. Tolerates sun with wet feet.
