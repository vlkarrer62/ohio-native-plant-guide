About This Plant

The Tree That Blooms From Its Bark

Eastern redbud is a small, often multi-stemmed tree of woodland edges, stream bluffs, and open limestone slopes, found across most of Ohio but scarce in the glaciated northeast. It grows twenty to thirty feet tall with a spreading, flat-topped crown and a trunk that often leans or forks low, and its twigs grow in a distinct zig-zag, bending at each bud. For most of the year it is a quiet understory tree; for two weeks in April it is the brightest thing in the woods.

The flowers come before the leaves, in tight clusters of small magenta-pink pea-flowers that sprout not only from the twigs but from old branches and the trunk itself. They are followed by broad, heart-shaped leaves with smooth edges and palmate veins, which turn clear yellow in fall, and by bunches of flat legume pods that ripen from green to purple-brown and rattle on the branches well into winter. The plant is a legume, a member of the pea family, and the flower shape — banner, wings, and keel — shows it at a glance.

Redbud opens its flowers when few other trees offer anything, and its deep pea-flowers are worked by queen bumblebees, mason bees, and carpenter bees strong enough to pry the keel open. It is also the chief Ohio host of Henry's elfin, a small spring hairstreak whose caterpillars feed on the flowers and young pods.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
In late March the buds along the bare twigs and trunk swell and show color, dotting the dark bark with beads of deep rose.
Mid-spring
Peak bloom in April — two or three weeks of magenta-pink clusters on leafless wood, worked by queen bumblebees, mason bees, and carpenter bees, with Henry's elfins laying eggs on the flower buds.
Late spring
Heart-shaped leaves unfold, bronze-tinged at first, as the spent flowers give way to clusters of thin, flat green pods.
Summer
A dense layer of broad leaves shades the woodland edge; leafcutter bees snip neat circles from their margins, and the pods fill out and redden.
Fall–winter
Leaves turn clear yellow and drop; bunches of purple-brown pods hang on through winter above the zig-zag twigs and dark, finely scaly bark.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Redbud's great gift to wildlife is its timing. It blooms in the lean weeks of April, when bumblebee queens are founding colonies and solitary bees are provisioning their first nests, and its closed pea-flowers reward the strong, long-tongued bees that can open them. Its leaves and flowers host a small but specialized set of caterpillars, and its seeds and branching cover serve birds through the rest of the year.

The neat round holes that appear in redbud leaves by midsummer are the work of leafcutter bees, which carry the pieces off to line their nest cells — a sign the tree is doing its job, not a problem to treat. Early ruby-throated hummingbirds returning in late April will also sip from the last of the flowers.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Redbud is one of the easiest native trees to grow and one of the best for a small yard, flowering within a few years of planting and staying a manageable size. It is not a long-lived tree — twenty to thirty years is typical in a garden — so plant it where its early bloom can be enjoyed and plan on letting a seedling take over in time. Plant it young; it grows a deep taproot and resents being moved once established.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Flowers From the Trunk

Most temperate trees bloom only on new growth at the twig tips, but redbud also flowers from dormant buds on old branches and on the trunk itself — a habit botanists call cauliflory, far more common in tropical trees like cacao. The buds persist in the bark for years, and each spring they burst into tufts of pink flowers directly from the wood. It is why an old redbud in bloom looks as though it has been dipped in color, trunk and all.

02

A Legume Without the Nodules

Redbud is in the pea family, and its flowers and pods are unmistakably those of a legume. But unlike clover, wild lupine, or false indigo, it does not form root nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Redbud belongs to an old, early-branching lineage of the family that split off before that partnership evolved — a reminder that "legume" and "nitrogen fixer" are not quite the same thing.

03

The Elfin of April

Henry's elfin is a small, dusky brown hairstreak that flies for only a few weeks in early spring. In Ohio its caterpillars feed mainly on redbud, eating the flower buds, blossoms, and young pods rather than the leaves, and the butterfly's single flight is timed to the tree's bloom. It spends the rest of the year as a chrysalis in the leaf litter below — one more reason to leave the leaves where they fall.

04

Redbuds and Judas Trees

The genus Cercis has a handful of species around the Northern Hemisphere, and the Mediterranean one, Cercis siliquastrum, is the "Judas tree" of old European legend. Ohio's redbud is its New World cousin. The name comes from the buds, which are a deep red-purple before they open into pink; settlers and naturalists noted that the flowers were sometimes eaten in spring salads, mentioned here as history, not as advice.

Ohio's Understory Trees

Meet Redbud's Neighbors

Ohio has only one native Cercis, but redbud shares the woodland edge and understory with several other small native trees. Each fills the layer beneath the oaks and tuliptrees with flowers, fruit, or fine structure, and together they make the difference between a forest and a woodland garden.
