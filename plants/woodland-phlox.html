About This Plant

The Blue Haze of the April Woods

Woodland phlox is one of the defining wildflowers of Ohio's rich deciduous forests. In late April and May, just as the trees leaf out overhead, it spreads a soft lavender-blue haze across the floor of moist ravines, stream terraces, and shaded slopes — sometimes in drifts of thousands, mixed with wild geranium, trillium, and the last of the spring ephemerals.

It is a low, loosely spreading perennial. Sterile shoots creep along the ground and root at the nodes, forming an open mat; from these rise slender, slightly sticky flowering stems with pairs of narrow leaves, each topped by an airy cluster of flowers about an inch across. Each flower has five flat, wedge-shaped petals — usually notched at the tip in Ohio plants — joined below into a narrow tube, and the whole drift carries a sweet, clean scent on warm afternoons.

Woodland phlox hides its nectar at the bottom of a long, narrow floral tube that only long-tongued insects can reach — so it is a specialist resource for swallowtail butterflies, clearwing moths, and the greater bee fly at a moment in spring when few other flowers offer them anything.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
Semi-evergreen mats green up early; flowering stems rise from the creeping shoots as the ephemerals bloom around them.
Mid–late spr.
Peak bloom from late April through May — fragrant lavender-blue clusters open just as the canopy closes overhead, visited by swallowtails, clearwings, and bee flies.
Early sum.
Flowering stems set small capsules and die back; the plant retreats to its low, leafy creeping shoots.
Summer–fall
The green mat persists quietly under shade, spreading slowly and rooting at the nodes where stems touch the soil.
Winter
Semi-evergreen rosettes hold through mild winters under leaf litter, ready to rise again in March.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Woodland phlox flowers in the narrow window between the spring ephemerals and the summer meadow, and its long floral tube sorts its visitors: the nectar goes to insects with the tongue to reach it. Watching a drift on a warm afternoon in May is one of the best ways to see Ohio's spring butterflies and moths at work.

The greater bee fly — a fuzzy, hovering fly with a long rigid proboscis that looks like a tiny hummingbird — is one of the most faithful spring visitors. Note that deer and rabbits readily eat phlox; in gardens with heavy browse pressure, plant it among sedges and wild ginger, which they avoid.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Woodland phlox wants what a good woodland floor gives it: dappled shade, loose humus-rich soil, and moisture in spring. It is easy and long-lived in those conditions and will spread into a loose drift over several years. It tolerates more sun in cool, moist soil, but resents hot, dry, compacted ground and will thin out there. Keep the fallen leaves on the bed — they are its natural mulch.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

Built for Long Tongues

The five flat petals of a phlox flower are a landing pad, but the reward sits at the bottom of a slender tube nearly an inch deep. Short-tongued bees cannot reach it, so the nectar is reserved for swallowtails, clearwing moths, and the greater bee fly, whose stiff, forward-pointing proboscis is exactly the right tool. In return, they carry pollen from drift to drift across the woods.

02

A Genus Almost Entirely American

The name Phlox is Greek for "flame," a reference to the hot colours of some species. Of roughly sixty-five species in the genus, all but one are native to North America — the exception lives in Siberia — and the garden phloxes grown around the world all descend from American wild plants, including this one.

03

Wild Sweet William

An old name for woodland phlox is wild sweet William, borrowed from the fragrance and the flat flower clusters of the European sweet William. The plant was among the first North American wildflowers sent to English gardens in the early 1700s by the Philadelphia botanist John Bartram, and it has been a shade-garden plant on both sides of the Atlantic ever since.

04

Notched or Not

Woodland phlox comes in two forms. The eastern subspecies, the one throughout most of Ohio, has petals with a distinct notch at the tip; the western subspecies, laphamii, has rounded, unnotched petals and a deeper blue. The two meet and blend around the western edge of the state, so a single drift can show both.

Genus Phlox

Meet the Phloxes

Phlox gives Ohio a native species for nearly every garden situation, and they sort by habitat and season. Woodland phlox, profiled above, is the spring plant of rich shade; its relatives take the summer border, the dry prairie, and the shaded slope.

Garden Phlox

Phlox paniculata

The tall, fragrant summer phlox of moist woodland edges and stream banks, with big domed heads of pink to lavender flowers in July and August. The wild parent of hundreds of garden cultivars, and a favourite of swallowtails and hummingbirds.

Prairie Phlox

Phlox pilosa

The sun-loving phlox of dry prairies and open oak woods, with downy stems and loose clusters of bright pink to lavender flowers in May and June — the bridge between the woodland species and the summer meadow.

Creeping Phlox

Phlox stolonifera

A true mat-forming phlox of Appalachian woodlands, lower and denser than woodland phlox, with rounded evergreen leaves and unnotched violet flowers in spring. Excellent on shaded banks where a tight groundcover is wanted.
