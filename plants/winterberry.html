About This Plant

The Holly That Drops Its Leaves

Winterberry is a holly, but not the kind on a Christmas card. Its leaves are thin, toothed, and spineless, and in autumn they turn a smoky yellow-black and fall — leaving a rounded, many-stemmed shrub of bare grey twigs. What stays behind is the point of the plant: dense whorls of glossy red berries pressed tight against the wood at every node, glowing against snow from October until the birds finally strip them in late winter.

It grows wild across Ohio in swamps, bog margins, wet woods, and the edges of ponds and slow streams, often in acidic, seasonally flooded ground where few other shrubs prosper. In gardens it is happiest in the same places: rain gardens, low wet corners, and pond edges, in as much sun as you can give it.

Winterberry is dioecious — male and female flowers grow on separate plants — so only females carry fruit, and only if a male winterberry blooms nearby at the same time. One male within about forty feet will pollinate six to ten females. The berries then hang on for months, a late-winter lifeline for more than forty kinds of birds.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
The last of the winter berries are eaten or drop. Leaves emerge late, in May — a plain, glossy green that reads as background all summer.
Early sum.
Tiny greenish-white flowers open in the leaf axils in June. Easy to miss, but small bees and flies find them and carry pollen from the male to the females.
Mid–late sum.
Hard green berries swell on the female plants, tucked under the leaves; the shrub is quietly building its winter show.
Fall
Berries colour scarlet in September and October. The leaves blacken with the first frosts and drop, and the fruit is suddenly the whole plant.
Winter
Peak season — bare twigs loaded with red berries against snow. Robins, waxwings, and bluebirds work through them as other food runs out, often finishing in February.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Winterberry's summer flowers are small and its insect crowd is modest, but its winter fruit makes it one of the most valuable bird shrubs in the state. The berries are low in fat compared with the oily fruits of spicebush and dogwood, so birds tend to leave them alone in autumn and return when better food is gone — which is exactly when a garden needs them most.

The June flowers are pollinated by small bees and flies, which is why a male plant has to be within their working range of the females. Hollies are among the host plants of Henry's elfin, a small brown butterfly of early spring, and the dense twiggy clumps give small birds cover through winter. The berries are not edible for people or pets — they belong to the birds.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Winterberry is a tough, slow, long-lived shrub with two real requirements: moist, acidic soil and a male plant. It tolerates wet feet, seasonal flooding, and heavy clay, and once settled it needs almost nothing. The one common failure in Ohio is alkaline soil, where the leaves yellow between the veins and the plant sulks; if your ground sits on limestone, a low spot with plenty of organic matter is the best bet.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

In This Garden: Two Females and a Southern Gentleman

Three winterberries grow here, and only two of them will ever carry fruit. The two red-berried plants are females. The third is a male cultivar called 'Southern Gentleman', and its whole job is to bloom at the same time as the females so that bees can move pollen between them. He will never have a berry on him — if you find a winterberry in the garden that looks bare in December, that is the one, and he is doing his job.

The pairing matters more than it sounds. Winterberries come in early- and late-flowering groups, and a male from the wrong group opens its flowers before the females are ready. 'Southern Gentleman' is the standard late bloomer, bred to pair with late-flowering females such as 'Winter Red' and 'Sparkleberry'.

02

Why the Berries Last So Long

Most native fruits are gone within weeks of ripening. Winterberry's persist for months, and the reason is nutritional: the berries are relatively low in fat and slightly bitter, so migrating birds fattening up in September pass them over for the oil-rich fruits of spicebush, dogwood, and Virginia creeper. Repeated freezing and thawing softens and sweetens them, and by January and February — when the rich food is long gone — flocks of robins and waxwings can strip a shrub in an afternoon. The plant, in effect, holds its fruit in reserve for the hungriest part of the year.

03

Black Alder, Michigan Holly, Fever Bush

Winterberry has collected a drawer full of names. "Black alder" comes from the way the leaves turn nearly black with the first hard frost, and from its habit of growing beside true alders in swamps. "Michigan holly" and "Canada holly" mark it as the holly of the north, hardy well beyond where the evergreen American holly can survive. "Fever bush" recalls a folk history of its bitter bark — mentioned here as history, not as a use. The Latin verticillata means "whorled," for the way the berries ring the twig at each node.

04

An Indiana Nursery and a Famous Pair

Many of the winterberries sold today trace back to Simpson Nursery in Vincennes, Indiana, which selected 'Winter Red' in the 1970s for its heavy, long-lasting fruit and later introduced 'Southern Gentleman' as its matching male. That one pairing did more than anything else to move winterberry out of the swamp and into ordinary gardens — and the reason it works is the same reason the wild plant works: a male and a female flowering in step, and a few bees to connect them.

Genus Ilex

Meet the Native Hollies

Ilex is a huge genus — several hundred species worldwide — but only a handful are native to the eastern United States, and fewer still to Ohio. All are dioecious, all carry berry-like fruit prized by birds, and they split neatly into evergreen and deciduous kinds. Winterberry, profiled above, is by far the most widespread and the most useful in an Ohio garden.
