About This Plant

The First Bloom of the Woods

Spicebush is the characteristic shrub of Ohio's rich, moist woodlands — a rounded, multi-stemmed understory plant of stream terraces, ravine bottoms, and shaded slopes, where it can form an open, waist-to-head-high layer beneath the trees. In late March and April, before its own leaves and before nearly anything else in the forest, its bare twigs are lined with small clusters of yellow flowers that light the still-grey understory with a soft, greenish-gold haze.

Every part of the plant is aromatic. Crush a leaf or scratch a twig and it releases a clean, spicy, citrus-like scent — the source of the common name, and of older names like wild allspice and Benjamin bush. The oval leaves are a fresh green through summer and turn a clear, glowing yellow in fall, when female plants carry glossy scarlet berries along the stems.

Spicebush is the primary host plant of the spicebush swallowtail, one of Ohio's signature butterflies, and a host for the promethea silkmoth. In fall its berries are unusually rich in fat — exactly the fuel that wood thrushes, veeries, and other migrating songbirds need before their long flights south.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
Peak bloom on bare wood — clusters of tiny yellow flowers open along the twigs in late March and April, feeding the first small bees and flies of the year.
Late spring
Fresh green leaves unfold as the flowers fade. Spicebush swallowtails begin laying eggs on the new foliage.
Summer
A full, rounded shrub in leaf. Swallowtail caterpillars fold leaves into shelters; green berries swell on female plants.
Fall
Berries ripen glossy scarlet in September and are stripped by thrushes and other migrants; the foliage turns a clear butter-yellow.
Winter
Bare, aromatic twigs with rounded flower buds already set at the nodes, ready to open at the first warm spell.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Spicebush works for wildlife at three different points in the year — early nectar and pollen, summer foliage for caterpillars, and high-energy fruit in fall — which is why a single shrub earns its place in a woodland garden.

The berries are the standout: their unusually high fat content makes them a preferred fuel for migrating thrushes, which can strip a female shrub in days during September. Because the plant is aromatic, deer generally browse it only lightly, and its dense, twiggy form gives nesting cover to small songbirds.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Spicebush is an easy, adaptable shrub once you give it what the woods give it: shade or part shade, and soil that does not dry out for long. It grows fuller and flowers more heavily with some sun, and stays looser and more open in deep shade. The one thing to plan is sex: plants are either male or female, and only females carry berries, so plant several — or buy a known female with a male nearby — if fruit is the goal.

LightPart shade is ideal; tolerates full sun with reliable moisture and deep shade at the cost of density

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

The Caterpillar With a Face

The spicebush swallowtail caterpillar is one of the most remarkable insects in Ohio. Young larvae look like bird droppings; older ones turn green and develop a pair of huge false eyespots that make them look, from the front, like a small snake. By day the caterpillar hides inside a leaf it has folded over and stitched shut with silk. A spicebush with folded leaves is not a damaged shrub — it is a nursery.

02

Two Sexes, One Shrub

Spicebush is dioecious: each plant is either male or female. Male flowers are slightly larger and showier in spring; only females go on to produce the scarlet berries. The fruit is a drupe with a single large seed, and it is unusually oily — up to a third of its weight is fat — which is why thrushes prize it so highly in migration.

03

The Forester's Shrub

Early settlers in the Ohio Valley learned to read spicebush as a soil indicator: a woodland full of it usually meant deep, rich, moist ground that would make good farmland. Dried and ground, the berries were used as an allspice substitute, and the twigs steeped as a fragrant tea — the origin of names like wild allspice and Benjamin bush. That history is mentioned here as background, not as a recommendation.

04

A Laurel in Ohio

Spicebush belongs to the laurel family, Lauraceae — the family of bay leaf, cinnamon, and avocado. Nearly all of its relatives are tropical; only two are native to Ohio, spicebush and sassafras, and both carry the family's aromatic oils. It is also one of the earliest-flowering woody plants in the state, opening with the spring peepers and well before the canopy leafs out.

Shrubs for the Shady Garden

Native shrubs are the most under-used layer in home gardens, and the one that does the most for nesting birds. Spicebush, profiled above, is the anchor for moist shade; these three companions extend the same understory across drier, wetter, and sunnier ground.

A low, mounding shrub of shaded ravines and rock outcrops with lacy, flat-topped white flower heads in midsummer. Smaller than spicebush and happy in drier shade, it fills the gap between shrub and perennial.

Witch Hazel

Spicebush's bookend: where spicebush opens the woodland year in March, witch hazel closes it with spidery yellow flowers in October and November. Larger and more tree-like, it tolerates the same moist shade.
