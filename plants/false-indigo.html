About This Plant

The Prairie's Blue Shrub

False indigo is a perennial that behaves like a shrub. Every spring it sends up a dense, rounded mound of smooth stems, three to four feet high and as wide, clothed in blue-green, three-part, clover-like leaves, and in late May and June tops them with upright spikes of indigo-blue pea flowers a foot long — one of the truest blues in the native garden. Beneath it is a taproot the size of a small tree's, which is why a plant, once settled, lives for fifty years and more.

After bloom the mound stays as a handsome blue-green shrub through summer while the flowers become fat, inflated seed pods that ripen to black and rattle in the wind — the "rattleweed" of country names. It is a legume, and like the clovers and lupines it fixes nitrogen from the air, feeding the ground it grows in. In the wild it is a plant of open woods, prairie edges, and stream gravel bars, uncommon in Ohio but native, and in gardens it is one of the most dependable perennials there is.

False indigo is a bumblebee plant — the flowers are built so that only a heavy bee can open them — a host plant for the wild indigo duskywing and several other butterflies, and a structural anchor that gives a prairie garden a shrub-like mass without a shrub. Its nitrogen-fixing roots feed the grasses around it.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
Thick, asparagus-like purple shoots push up in April and unfurl fast into a blue-green mound; the plant reaches full size in a month.
Late spring
Peak bloom in late May and early June — foot-long spikes of indigo pea flowers over the mound, worked by bumblebees for two to three weeks.
Summer
A neat blue-green shrub through the heat, untouched by drought; green pods swell where the flowers were and duskywing caterpillars feed in folded leaves.
Fall
Pods ripen to charcoal-black and rattle with loose seed; the foliage turns dark and the whole plant often blackens after frost.
Winter
Black pods stand on stiff stems through the snow — or the whole top breaks off at the crown and tumbles like a tumbleweed, scattering seed as it goes.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

A pea flower is a locked box. The two lower petals are pressed together around the stamens and nectar, and only a bee heavy enough to force them apart — a bumblebee or a large carpenter bee — gets in and gets dusted with pollen. Smaller bees rob nectar from the side or go elsewhere. The leaves, meanwhile, are the food of a small guild of legume-eating butterflies.

The wild indigo duskywing is the plant's specialist: a small dark skipper-like butterfly whose caterpillars fold the leaves into shelters and feed on little else. The foliage is bitter with alkaloids and is never browsed by deer or rabbits, which makes false indigo one of the most reliable plants in a heavily browsed garden.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

False indigo is slow to start and then permanent. A young plant spends its first two or three years building a taproot and looks unimpressive; from then on it is a fixture that shrugs off drought, poor soil, neglect, and deer, and it will outlive the gardener. The one rule is to plant it where it will stay — the taproot makes it nearly impossible to move — and to give it full sun and room for a four-foot mound.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

The Dye That Wasn't

True indigo, the blue dye of denim, comes from a tropical plant that would not grow in the colonies, so settlers and enslaved dyers tried the native wild indigos instead. The plants gave a blue — but a weak, fugitive one, and the name "false indigo" records the disappointment. The genus name Baptisia comes from the Greek for "to dip," as in dyeing. The flowers, ironically, are a far better blue than the dye ever was.

02

Rattle Pods

The seed pods swell into fat, pointed capsules two inches long and ripen from green to charcoal black, and as they dry the seeds come loose inside and rattle in the wind. Children have used them as rattles for as long as anyone remembers — rattleweed and rattlebush are old names — and florists cut the black-podded stems for arrangements. In the garden they are the plant's second season, standing through winter after the flowers are months gone.

03

A Root Like a Tree

Dig up an old false indigo and you find a woody taproot as thick as a wrist, going down several feet — the reason it survives drought that kills everything around it, the reason it cannot be moved, and the reason it can live half a century in one spot. On that root, as on all legumes, are the nodules where bacteria turn air into nitrogen fertiliser, so a false indigo quietly feeds the grasses and flowers planted next to it.

04

Tumbleweed

In late fall the whole top of a false indigo often breaks cleanly from the crown, and the stiff, rounded mass of stems and pods rolls off across the field in the wind, shedding seed as it goes — one of the few eastern plants that behaves as a true tumbleweed. It is also why the crown itself, safe underground with its taproot, comes back the next spring as if nothing had happened.

Genus Baptisia

Meet the Wild Indigos

Baptisia — the wild indigos — are long-lived, taprooted legumes of the prairies and open woods, all with clover-like leaves, pea flowers, and inflated pods. False indigo, profiled above, is the blue one; its relatives give the same shrubby structure in white, cream, and yellow.
