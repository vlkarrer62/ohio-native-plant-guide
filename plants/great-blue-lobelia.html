About This Plant

The Bumblebee's Blue

True blue is rare in the late-summer garden, and great blue lobelia has it. It is a stout, leafy, unbranched perennial of wet meadows, stream banks, ditches, and seeps, two to four feet tall, with alternate, toothed, lance-shaped leaves and, from August into October, a dense spike of inch-long flowers in a clear, deep blue. Each flower is a tube split into two lips: two narrow petals raised above and three broad ones spread below, the lower lip striped white at the throat.

It is the blue counterpart of the scarlet cardinal flower, the same genus, the same wet ground, the same season — but where cardinal flower is built for hummingbirds, great blue lobelia is built for bumblebees, and its spikes hum with them on warm September afternoons. It is a short-lived perennial that keeps its place by seeding freely into open, moist soil.

Great blue lobelia is a bumblebee plant above all: the wide lower lip is a landing platform, the tube is sized for a long-tongued bee, and the bloom comes in the weeks when new bumblebee queens are feeding up for winter. It carries the wet garden into October after most of its neighbours have finished.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
A low basal rosette that overwintered green begins to grow; new seedlings appear on bare, damp ground from last year's seed.
Early sum.
The leafy stem rises through the wet meadow, unbranched and upright, still green and unremarkable among the summer bloomers.
Late summer
Peak bloom from August into September — the spike opens from the bottom up over several weeks, a column of blue worked by bumblebees.
Fall
Bloom continues into October, among the last flowers of the wet garden. Seed capsules ripen along the spent spike and shake out tiny seed.
Winter
The stem browns and stands; the basal rosette stays green at the ground, and the seed waits in the mud for spring.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

The flower shape sorts the visitors. Bumblebees are the main pollinators, landing on the lower lip and pushing into the tube; the anthers, fused into a ring around the style, brush pollen on their backs as they go. Large sweat bees and leafcutter bees follow, and hummingbirds sample it on their way to the cardinal flowers.

Timing is its gift: the bloom overlaps the weeks when new bumblebee queens forage heavily before hibernating, so a patch in September is feeding next year's colonies. The foliage contains bitter alkaloids and is avoided by deer and rabbits.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Great blue lobelia is easy where the ground is reliably moist, and tolerates more shade and heavier soil than most sun perennials. The key to keeping it is understanding that individual plants are short-lived — two to four years — and that the colony persists by seed. Leave the spent spikes, keep some open, damp soil around them, and it will renew itself indefinitely. In dry beds it fades out after a season or two.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

A Flower Built Like a Bee Trap

Look into a lobelia flower and the design is plain. The three lower petals make a platform sized for a bumblebee; the two upper petals are split apart to let the bee's back through; and above them, the five stamens are fused into a tube around the style. As the bee pushes in for nectar, that tube dusts pollen onto its back, and on the next flower the emerging stigma scrapes it off. Butterflies can drink from it, but only a bee of the right size works the mechanism.

02

A Name from a Failed Cure

The species name is a relic of the 1700s. Colonial officials learned that Indigenous healers used the plant's root in medicine, and in the 1760s the British superintendent Sir William Johnson sent it to Europe as a supposed cure for syphilis, which is how Linnaeus came to name it siphilitica. European physicians tested it and found it did nothing of the kind, but the name stuck. The plant contains real and toxic alkaloids, and is mentioned here as history, not as a remedy.

03

Blue Meets Red

Where great blue lobelia and cardinal flower grow together on the same stream bank, they occasionally cross, and the offspring — Lobelia × speciosa — come out in purples and magentas between the two parents. Plant breeders have used that hybrid to make the purple garden lobelias sold in nurseries. In a wet native garden the two species make a better pair kept distinct: scarlet spikes for the hummingbirds, blue for the bees, side by side in September.

04

A Short Life, Many Children

Great blue lobelia is a perennial that behaves like a biennial. Each plant lives only a few years, so the colony survives by seed: the capsules split in fall and shake out thousands of dust-fine seeds that need bare, damp soil and light to germinate. A wet garden that is mulched heavily or planted wall to wall will lose it; one with some open mud at the edge of the sedges will have it forever, moving a little each year.

Genus Lobelia

Meet the Lobelias

Lobelia gives Ohio a set of two-lipped wildflowers sorted neatly by habitat and colour. Great blue lobelia, profiled above, is the blue of the wet meadow; its relatives take the same wet ground in scarlet, the dry prairie in pale blue, and the roadsides as a small annual.
