About This Plant

The Bird Shrub

Elderberry is the big, loose, generous shrub of Ohio's wet places — ditch banks, stream sides, fencerows, the low corner of a field, anywhere the ground stays damp. It grows fast from a spreading root system, sending up arching canes with large, opposite, compound leaves of five to eleven toothed leaflets, and in a few years makes a thicket six to twelve feet high and as wide.

In June and early July every cane ends in a broad, flat plate of tiny creamy-white flowers, six to ten inches across and faintly sweet-scented, so that a stand of elderberry looks laid with lace. By late August those plates have bent under the weight of hundreds of small, glossy, purple-black berries — and for the next few weeks the shrub is the busiest feeding station in the neighbourhood.

Elderberry is one of the most valuable bird plants in the eastern states: more than forty species eat the fruit, from thrushes and catbirds to waxwings and grosbeaks, and the dense canes give nesting cover. Its pollen-rich flowers feed bees, flies, and beetles, and its hollow stems are nest sites for small native bees.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
Among the first shrubs to leaf out, in April; new canes shoot up from the roots and the old ones branch.
Early sum.
Peak bloom in June and early July — broad, flat, creamy plates of flowers on every cane, busy with bees, hover flies, and beetles gathering pollen.
Midsummer
Green berries swell and the clusters begin to droop; the shrub is a dense green mass that hides catbird and cardinal nests.
Late summer
Berries ripen purple-black in late August and September and are stripped within weeks by thrushes, catbirds, robins, waxwings, and migrants passing through.
Fall–winter
Leaves drop early with little colour. The pale, pithy canes stand through winter, and small bees overwinter inside cut or broken stems.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Elderberry flowers produce pollen but almost no nectar, so their visitors are the pollen-eaters: small bees, hover flies, and beetles that swarm over the flat plates in June. The berries are the main event. Few native shrubs are eaten by so many kinds of birds, and the timing — ripening just as fall migration begins — makes them a critical stopover food.

The hollow, pith-filled stems are as valuable as the fruit: small carpenter bees and mason bees excavate the soft pith of cut or broken canes to nest and overwinter, so a stand left unpruned until spring is a bee hotel. Deer browse young growth but rarely trouble an established thicket.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Elderberry is about as easy as a native shrub gets, provided it has moisture. Give it damp ground and sun and it will grow several feet a year, flower in its second or third season, and fruit heavily thereafter. The two things to plan for are space — it suckers into a thicket — and the fact that individual canes are short-lived, so the shrub looks best with old wood cut out every few years.

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

A Feast for Forty Birds

Elderberries ripen in the last weeks of August, exactly when the first songbirds begin moving south and need to put on fat. The berries are small, soft, and borne in the hundreds on each cluster, so a flock of waxwings or a family of catbirds can feed without effort. Ornithologists have recorded well over forty bird species eating them. In a native garden, few single plants feed as many mouths in as short a time.

02

Flowers Without Nectar

An elderberry in bloom hums with insects, but almost none of them are drinking. The tiny flowers produce abundant pollen and little or no nectar, which shapes the crowd: pollen-collecting bees, hover flies whose larvae eat aphids, and beetles that chew the pollen where they stand. Butterflies mostly pass it by. It is a reminder that "pollinator plant" means different things to different insects.

03

The Hollow Stem

Elderberry canes are filled with soft white pith that is easily pushed out, leaving a hollow tube. Children have made pea-shooters and whistles from them for centuries, and the genus name Sambucus comes from the sambuca, an ancient stringed instrument once said to be made of elder wood. Small carpenter bees discovered the same property long ago: they hollow the pith of broken stems to lay their eggs, which is why an unpruned elderberry is a bee nursery.

04

Poison and Pie

Every part of the plant, including the raw berries, contains compounds that release cyanide when eaten, and the leaves, stems, and unripe fruit can make people ill. Cooking destroys them, and cooked elderberries have gone into pies, syrups, and wine for as long as there have been settlers in Ohio — the flowers into fritters and cordials. That history is mentioned here as background, not as a recommendation; in this garden the berries belong to the birds.

Native Shrubs

Shrubs for Wet Ground

Wet soil defeats most garden shrubs, but Ohio has a whole set of natives that want it. Elderberry, profiled above, is the fastest and the best for birds; these three companions extend the wet shrub layer into standing water, deeper shade, and winter interest.

Buttonbush

Cephalanthus occidentalis

A shrub that will grow with its roots in a pond, carrying perfect white globes of fragrant flowers in July that draw swallowtails, skippers, and bees. Ducks eat the seed balls. The wettest-tolerant shrub in the state.

Silky Dogwood

Cornus amomum

A rounded dogwood of stream banks and swamp edges with flat clusters of white flowers in June, porcelain-blue berries in late summer, and reddish twigs. Tolerates more shade than elderberry and holds banks against erosion.

Winterberry

Ilex verticillata

A deciduous holly of swamps and wet woods whose female plants hold bright red berries on bare twigs all winter, feeding robins and waxwings when little else is left. Needs a male nearby to fruit.
