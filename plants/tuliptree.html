About This Plant

A Tulip in the Treetops

The tuliptree is the giant of Ohio's forests — the tallest hardwood in eastern North America, regularly over a hundred feet and occasionally past a hundred and fifty, on a trunk so straight and clear of branches that the old-growth specimens look like columns. It grows fast in deep, moist, well-drained soil in coves, lower slopes, and bottomlands, and it is common across the state, especially in the hills of the east and south.

Two things identify it at any distance. The leaf has four lobes and a broad tip that looks squared off and notched, as if the point had been cut away with scissors — a shape no other tree shares — and it turns a clear butter-yellow in fall. And in late May the canopy carries thousands of two-inch tulip-shaped flowers, greenish-yellow with a band of orange at the base of each petal, held upright at the branch tips and full of nectar. Most people never see one on the tree; they find them fallen on the sidewalk.

Tuliptree is the primary host tree of the eastern tiger swallowtail and of the tuliptree silkmoth, its flowers are a major nectar source for bees in the weeks after the spring flowers fade, and its winter seed cones feed finches and cardinals. One mature tree does the work of a whole flower bed — a hundred feet up.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Spring
Duck-bill buds open in April into the four-lobed leaves; the crown fills quickly with fresh, glossy green.
Late spring
Peak bloom in late May and early June — tulip-shaped flowers by the thousand in the upper canopy, dripping nectar for bees, and the first swallowtail eggs laid on the leaves.
Summer
Dense shade; tiger swallowtail and silkmoth caterpillars feed on the leaves, and cicadas and katydids sing from the branches.
Fall
Leaves turn a clear yellow in October — among the best fall colour of any large native tree — while the cone-like seed heads ripen and stand upright on the bare twigs.
Winter
The pale seed cones shed winged seeds through winter to finches, cardinals, and squirrels; the straight grey trunk and candelabra branching are unmistakable.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

A tuliptree feeds wildlife at every level. The flowers are among the most nectar-rich of any native tree — each can hold a teaspoon — and bees, from bumblebees to honeybees, work them for a fortnight. The leaves raise a short list of large, spectacular caterpillars. The seeds feed birds through winter, and the sheer size of a mature tree makes it a nesting and roosting site for everything from hawks to warblers.

The tree's flowers are a recognised honey source — beekeepers prize the dark, rich tulip-poplar honey of late spring — and hummingbirds visit them on migration. The canopy is a singing perch for the cicadas and katydids profiled in the wildlife directory. Deer browse seedlings but leave the tree alone once it is above their reach.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

The only real question with a tuliptree is whether there is room. It grows three feet a year in good soil and does not stop, so it belongs in a large yard, a park, a school ground, or a woodland edge — never near a house foundation or under wires. Given space, deep soil, and sun, it is fast, straight, long-lived, and nearly trouble-free, and it is the quickest native tree to produce a real canopy.

LightFull sun; seedlings tolerate light shade but the tree wants open sky
SoilDeep, rich, well-drained loam is ideal; tolerates clay if not waterlogged; slightly acidic to neutral
MoistureMedium to moist; not drought-tolerant, and drops leaves early in a dry summer
HabitTall, straight, pyramidal when young, broad-crowned with age; 70–100 ft and 40 ft wide, much more in old age
SpacePlant at least 30 ft from buildings and clear of overhead lines; brittle branches drop in ice and wind
MaintenanceLow; aphids and their honeydew are common but harmless; no pruning beyond training a single leader
WildlifeLeave fallen leaves beneath it for overwintering swallowtail chrysalises and silkmoth cocoons

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

The Swallowtail's Tree

The eastern tiger swallowtail, Ohio's big yellow butterfly, lays most of its eggs on two trees — wild black cherry and tuliptree — and a neighbourhood with tuliptrees in it raises far more swallowtails than any flower garden can. The same leaves feed the tuliptree silkmoth, a big orange-brown giant silk moth whose caterpillars grow to the length of a finger. Neither does the tree any visible harm; a hundred-foot canopy has leaves to spare.

02

Canoewood

The straight, clear, lightweight trunk made tuliptree the canoe tree of the eastern woodlands. Native peoples hollowed single logs into dugouts, and the settlers followed: Daniel Boone is said to have carried his family down the Ohio in a sixty-foot tuliptree dugout. The wood is still called yellow poplar in the lumber trade, though the tree is a magnolia and no relation to the poplars, and it goes into everything from furniture frames to organ pipes.

03

A Genus Split by an Ocean

There are exactly two tuliptrees in the world: this one, across eastern North America, and the Chinese tuliptree of central China, half a world away and nearly identical. They are the survivors of a genus that once grew across the Northern Hemisphere, before ice ages wiped it from Europe and split what was left. Ohio's woods hold a dozen such pairs — magnolias, sassafras, witch hazel — that have their only close relatives in East Asia.

04

The Nectar Tree

A tuliptree flower makes so much nectar that it pools in the cup and drips — beekeepers reckon a single big tree can yield several pounds of honey — and for the two weeks it blooms it is the largest nectar source in the forest, arriving in the gap between the spring ephemerals and the summer meadow. Because the flowers open a hundred feet up, most of that traffic is invisible from the ground. The evidence is the sticky sidewalk beneath.

Family Magnoliaceae

Meet the Magnolias

The tuliptree is a magnolia, and Ohio is the northern edge of a small group of native magnolias that share its large leaves, big solitary flowers, and cone-like fruit. Tuliptree, profiled above, is by far the commonest and the only one for a sunny yard; its relatives are trees of the southern hills.

Cucumber Magnolia

Magnolia acuminata

Ohio's only true magnolia found across much of the state — a tall forest tree with large oval leaves, inconspicuous greenish flowers in May, and knobbly pink-red fruit that looks like a small cucumber. Hardy, handsome, and underplanted.

Umbrella Magnolia

Magnolia tripetala

A small tree of shaded ravines in the southern hills with two-foot leaves clustered like an umbrella at the branch tips, big cream flowers with a strong scent in May, and red fruit cones. Tropical-looking and rare in the wild.

Bigleaf Magnolia

Magnolia macrophylla

The largest simple leaf of any North American tree — up to three feet long — on a small tree of a few sheltered ravines in southern Ohio, with foot-wide white flowers in June. At the northern limit of its range and protected in the state.
