About This Plant

Foam of the Forest Floor

Foamflower is the groundcover of Ohio's rich, moist woodlands — the cool ravine slopes and shaded stream terraces where wild ginger, trillium, and the spring ephemerals grow. It forms a low, spreading carpet of heart-shaped, shallowly lobed leaves, a little like a small maple leaf, softly hairy and often marked with dark maroon along the veins. The leaves are semi-evergreen, holding through mild winters and turning bronze-red in the cold.

In late April and May, just as the canopy closes overhead, every crown sends up a slender leafless stalk six to twelve inches tall, topped by a narrow spike of dozens of tiny white flowers. Each is a five-pointed star with ten long, thread-like stamens standing well clear of the petals, so that the whole spike looks fuzzy and soft — the foam of the name. A drift in bloom in a dark wood seems to glow. The plant spreads by slender runners that root at the tip, so a single crown becomes a colony in a few years.

Foamflower is the living mulch of the shade garden — a carpet that holds soil on slopes, keeps the ground cool and moist, and gives spring's small bees and flower flies a nectar source in the weeks between the ephemerals and the summer woods. It does this without ever needing to be mown, divided, or sprayed.

Seasonal Interest

A Year in the Life

Early spring
The overwintered leaves green up from bronze and fresh ones unfold; flower stalks rise from every crown among the ephemerals.
Mid–late spr.
Peak bloom from late April through May — frothy white spikes over the whole colony for three or four weeks, visited by small bees and flower flies.
Early sum.
Spikes set their tiny tiara-shaped capsules and fade; the leaves take over as a neat, even green carpet under the closed canopy.
Summer–fall
Runners creep outward and root at the tips, widening the drift. Leaves stay clean and full through summer in moist shade.
Winter
Semi-evergreen; the leaves flush bronze and maroon and persist under snow and leaf litter until spring.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits

Foamflower's tiny, open flowers suit small insects: short-tongued native bees, mining bees, and the flower flies that hover along woodland paths in May. It fills a real gap — blooming after the ephemerals fade and before the summer woodland flowers open — and its dense, cool carpet is the ground-level habitat for the small things that feed everything else.

The carpet matters as much as the flowers: a foamflower drift keeps the soil beneath it cool and damp through summer, which is exactly what firefly larvae, ground beetles, and woodland salamanders need. Deer and rabbits generally leave it alone.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Foamflower is one of the easiest native groundcovers for shade, provided the ground is what it would find in the woods: loose, leafy, humus-rich, and never baked dry. Give it that and it needs nothing — no cutting back, no fertiliser, no division except to make more plants. It spreads at a polite pace by runners, fills in between larger perennials without smothering them, and can be lifted and moved in spring or fall whenever a new corner needs covering.

LightPart shade to full shade; tolerates morning sun in cool, moist soil, scorches in hot afternoon sun
SoilRich, loose, humusy, well-drained; neutral to slightly acidic; keep the fallen leaves on it
MoistureMedium to moist; not drought-tolerant — the one thing that will thin a drift is dry summer shade
HabitLow mat spreading by runners; 4–6 in of foliage, 6–12 in in bloom; a crown covers a foot or more in a few years
SpacingAbout 12 in apart for a carpet in two seasons
MaintenanceEssentially none; remove spent spikes for tidiness if wanted; divide runners in spring or fall to spread
Watch forSlugs on new growth; crown rot in heavy, waterlogged soil

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

A Tiara of Seed

The genus name Tiarella means "little tiara," and the reason is hidden in the seed pod. After the flowers fade, each one leaves a small capsule made of two unequal halves, the larger curving up over the smaller like the front of a jewelled headdress. Linnaeus named the plant for it in 1753. The species name cordifolia, "heart-leaved," describes the foliage, and "foamflower" describes everything in between.

02

Runners Across the Forest Floor

Ohio's foamflower spreads by stolons — thin runners that creep over the leaf litter and root where they touch down, the way a strawberry does. That is what makes it a groundcover. Farther south, in the Appalachians, the same species grows in tight, non-running clumps, and botanists have long argued whether those southern plants are a variety or a separate species, Tiarella wherryi. For a gardener the difference is practical: the running form carpets, the clumping form stays put.

03

Foam in the Dark Woods

Foamflower blooms in the narrow window when the tree canopy is closing and the woodland floor is going dark. Its tiny flowers would be invisible on their own; what makes the spikes show is the ten long white stamens standing out from each one, which scatter light and make the whole spike look soft and luminous. In the dim May woods a drift reads as a low white mist — a display built for the shade, and for the small insects that hunt through it.

04

The Garden Cross

Foamflower has a famous hybrid child. In 1912 the French nursery of Lemoine crossed it with its cousin the coral bells, Heuchera, and produced ×Heucherella — a cross between two different genera, something rare in the plant world. Modern garden heucherellas, with their patterned leaves and pink foam spikes, all descend from that cross. The wild foamflower is still the better choice for a woodland carpet: it runs, and it feeds the bees that the hybrids often do not.

Saxifrage Family

Telling Them Apart

Foamflower belongs to the saxifrage family, and Ohio's woods hold several relatives with the same rounded, lobed leaves and airy spikes of small flowers. They are easy to confuse in leaf and easy to separate in bloom — and one of them is a garden hybrid that often gets sold under the foamflower name.

The native coral bells — a clump of rounded, marbled leaves and tall, wiry stalks of tiny greenish-white bells in early summer. Drier and rockier ground than foamflower, and it stays in a clump. The parent of the garden heucheras.

Foamy Bells

×Heucherella

The nursery cross between foamflower and coral bells: patterned, often bronze or lime leaves and pink or white foam spikes. Handsome, but a sterile hybrid that does not run and offers little to insects. Often sold where the wild plant should be.
