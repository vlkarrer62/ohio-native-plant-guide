About This Plant

The Prairie Turned Vertical

Most prairie wildflowers spread their bloom outward — a daisy face, a flat-topped cluster. Blazing star concentrates it upward: a dense, unbranched spike packed with small feathery flower heads that open in a slow purple wave down the stem. In a planting, the effect is architectural — a rhythm of vertical strokes rising out of mounded and grass-textured neighbors.

The timing matters as much as the shape. Blazing star reaches peak bloom in mid to late summer, a stretch when many early perennials have finished and the fall asters haven't started. That position in the season makes it a reliable bridge in the nectar sequence, often busy with bumble bees, butterflies, and other visitors during the hottest weeks of the year.

Blazing star's late-summer bloom often overlaps with the period when monarchs are moving through Ohio, and patches in flower are a frequently recommended nectar stop in monarch-focused plantings. The dense spikes offer many small nectar-rich florets in one landing zone — an efficient refueling stand for a butterfly with a long way to go.

Wildlife Support

Who Visits — and Why It Matters

Each spike carries many small flower heads, and each head holds a cluster of tubular disc florets — so a single stem offers dozens of nectar stops in one vertical line. Larger visitors like bumble bees and swallowtails can walk the spike floret to floret rather than flying between blooms, which is part of why blazing star tends to hold visitors for long, unhurried stretches.

The relationship runs deeper than nectar. A few moths are closely tied to the genus — the bleeding flower moth, for example, is reported to develop on Liatris flowers and seeds — and in fall the ripe seedheads become a standing bird feeder, one more reason to leave the stalks up through winter.

Care & Cultivation

Growing Conditions

Blazing star grows from a corm — a bulb-like storage structure — and stays where you plant it, forming a slowly widening clump rather than a running colony. The main thing it asks for is sun and drainage; in many gardens, soggy winter soil is harder on the corms than summer drought.

LightFull sun for the strongest, most upright spikes; tends to lean or thin in shade
SoilAverage to lean, well-drained soils; dense blazing star is the most tolerant of moisture-retentive sites
MoistureMedium once established; species range from dry-site to moist-meadow (see the species guide below)
Height2–5 feet depending on species and site; taller spikes may lean in rich soil
SpreadClump-forming from corms; self-sows modestly where seedheads are left standing
Wildlife pressureRabbits often browse young shoots, and voles sometimes dig corms — new plantings may need protection in some gardens
Winter careLeave stalks standing for seed-eating birds and stem structure; cut back in early spring

Did You Know?

The Stories Behind This Plant

01

It Blooms Upside Down

Most spike-flowering plants open from the bottom up — think of a lupine or a foxglove climbing toward an unopened tip. Blazing star does the opposite: the spike opens from the top down, the crown flowering first while the lower buds wait their turn. It's one of the easiest field marks in the genus, and one of the more unusual bloom sequences among familiar garden perennials.

The practical effect is a long, orderly bloom. As the wave of open florets descends the stem week by week, the spike keeps presenting fresh flowers — and fresh nectar — at a steadily moving front, rather than spending itself all at once.

02

No Petals — Just a Thousand Threads

Look closely and there isn't a petal in the usual sense anywhere on the spike. Blazing star belongs to the aster family, but unlike a daisy it carries no ray florets at all — every flower head is a tight cluster of small tubular disc florets. The feathery texture comes from the florets' long, thread-like styles, which extend well beyond the flower and curl outward.

Those threads are the whole show. En masse they give the spike its soft, flame-like fuzz — the source of both common names, "blazing star" and the older florists' name gayfeather.

03

The Corm Is the Strategy

Underground, blazing star keeps its reserves in a corm — a firm, bulb-like base that stores energy from season to season. On the historic prairie, that structure was insurance: drought, fire, and grazing could take the top growth, and the corm would send it back up. In the garden, the same trait makes established plants notably drought-tolerant and long-lived in lean, well-drained soil.

It also explains blazing star's tidy manners. Unlike rhizomatous spreaders that weave outward through a planting, a corm stays put — the clump widens slowly, and new plants arrive mainly by seed. The one caveat: voles find the corms as nourishing as the plant does, and in some gardens new plantings benefit from protection.

04

A Prairie Native in the World's Flower Markets

Blazing star is one of the few North American prairie wildflowers to have become a commercial cut flower grown around the world. The florist trade knows it as liatris or gayfeather, and it's cultivated far beyond its native range for exactly the traits gardeners value: a strong straight stem, a long vase life, and that top-down bloom, which means the visible tip of a cut spike is always the freshest part.

So a stem in a supermarket bouquet and a wand in an Ohio prairie remnant are the same plant leading two very different lives — one of the quieter ways a tallgrass native has traveled the globe.

Ohio Species

Ohio Blazing Star Species

Several species of blazing star are native to Ohio, spanning a wide moisture gradient — from moist prairie swales to dry gravel and limestone slopes. They share the vertical spike and top-down bloom; they differ in stature, head size, and the sites they call home.

Flagship Species

Dense Blazing Star

Liatris spicata

The species most often grown in gardens and the standard of the cut-flower trade. Tall, tightly packed spikes of purple; the most tolerant of moist and average garden soils, at home in prairie swales and rain garden edges. The benchmark for the genus in cultivation.

3–5 ft Full sun Moist – medium Garden standard

Dry sites · Late bloom

Rough Blazing Star

Liatris aspera

A dry-prairie species with a looser, more open spike — the flower heads read as distinct rounded buttons spaced along the stem. Tends to bloom later than dense blazing star, extending the genus's season, and thrives in sandy or gravelly soil where richer plantings struggle.

2–4 ft Full sun Dry – medium Button-like heads

Compact · Lean soils

Cylindrical Blazing Star

Liatris cylindracea

The smallest of Ohio's blazing stars — a fine-textured plant of dry, often limestone-influenced prairies and gravel slopes, with relatively few, neatly cylindrical flower heads. Suited to rock gardens, lean hell-strips, and other tight, well-drained spots where taller species would flop.

1–2 ft Full sun Dry · well-drained Smallest species

Savanna · Bold heads

Savanna Blazing Star

Liatris scariosa

A blazing star of savannas and open woodland edges, carrying fewer but noticeably larger flower heads on the spike. Its looser architecture and tolerance of light, dappled conditions make it the family's candidate for the sunnier edge of a woodland planting.

2–4 ft Sun – light shade Dry – medium Large heads
